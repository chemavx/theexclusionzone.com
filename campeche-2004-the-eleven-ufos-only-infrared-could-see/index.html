Campeche 2004: The Eleven UFOs Only Infrared Could See

In March 2004 a Mexican Air Force plane recorded eleven lights on infrared that no one could see with the naked eye, and the Secretary of Defense handed the footage to television. The video itself holds the data that explains what they were.

ChemaVX

On March 5, 2004, a Mexican Air Force aircraft on a routine patrol against drug smuggling recorded a string of brilliant lights on its infrared camera that the crew could not see out of the window. At one point there were eleven. Two months later the footage, handed over by Mexico's own Secretary of Defense to a television journalist, went public, and "eleven UFOs over Campeche" became a worldwide story.

It is an unusual case: a military released sensor footage of unidentified objects on its own initiative. It is also one of the better-solved ones, because the video itself carries the data needed to work out where the camera was pointing. What makes Campeche worth revisiting is not only what the lights were, but how that was established, who got it wrong along the way, and which questions are still open more than twenty years later.

A drug patrol and a camera that sees heat

The aircraft was a Merlin C-26A belonging to Mexico's Secretariat of National Defense (SEDENA). According to the analysis Robert Sheaffer published in the September/October 2004 issue of Skeptical Inquirer, it was flying at about 3,500 meters (roughly 11,500 feet) over the states of Chiapas and Campeche when its infrared system began recording very bright points of light.

That system was a Star SAFIRE II, built by FLIR Systems of Portland, Oregon. It does not record visible light but heat: it detects radiation in the 3-to-5-micron band and builds its picture from differences in temperature and emissivity across the scene. On a display like that, anything hotter than its background looks as if it were glowing on its own.

That technical detail is the key to the whole case. The Campeche lights could not be seen with the naked eye; they existed only in the infrared. For those who argued the objects were anomalous, that was proof of something extraordinary. For infrared specialist John Lester Miller, whom Sheaffer consulted, it meant the opposite. "These systems are specifically designed to detect objects that cannot be seen by the human eye," he explained. "If the eye could see everything that the IR sensor can, then it would be far cheaper and more effective to put a few privates in the aircraft with binoculars."

From a military archive to prime-time television

The footage was not leaked. It was handed over by General Clemente Ricardo Vega García, head of SEDENA, to Jaime Maussan, a broadcaster with the Mexican network Televisa. The general explained his decision on Carlos Loret de Mola's radio show, as reported by the Mexico City daily La Jornada on May 13, 2004: he "saw no point in leaving that recorded there, stored away," and decided "it should be given to someone who has been working on this all his life, so that he would make good use of it" (our translation).

Maussan presented the video at a press conference on May 11. The story of eleven UFOs over Mexico was picked up by the international wires and ran around the world. La Jornada itself noted that in many places it had become an object of ridicule.

The choice of recipient shaped everything that followed. Sheaffer, an avowed skeptic, describes Maussan as a broadcaster known for sensational promotion of supposed mysteries, and notes that columnist Raúl Trejo Delarbre made the same point in La Crónica de Hoy on May 17: anyone wanting an objective evaluation of the video would not have started there.

The scientists who asked for the data

The least-known part of the story concerns two researchers at the Institute of Nuclear Sciences of Mexico's National Autonomous University (UNAM): Rafael Navarro González, described by La Jornada as the only Mexican specialist on NASA's Mars exploration project, and Julio Herrera Velázquez. Looking at the images, they suggested the lights might be ball lightning, a rare form of atmospheric electrical discharge, and considered them scientifically valuable for exactly that reason: ball lightning, they said, had never been observed in such conditions.

To test the idea they asked SEDENA for specifics: the frequency of the radars, the aircraft's altitude and speed, and how fast the luminous spheres were moving. According to the same newspaper, they received a single sentence in reply: the information requested was classified. The video had been given to a television broadcaster; the technical data needed to analyze it was withheld from the country's national university.

The ball-lightning idea did not survive, and it was not the only premature explanation. Sheaffer lists several from those weeks: the Urania Astronomical Society of Morelos told the newspaper El Universal the objects might be weather balloons; astronomer José de la Herrán suggested meteor fragments. None fit what the video shows. Sheaffer is as critical of these answers as of the believers' claims. The honest response, he argues, would have been to say there was not yet enough information to know what the lights were.

What the video itself says

The answer came from the simplest possible source: the data the sensor overlays on the screen. The Star SAFIRE II continuously records where the camera is pointing relative to the aircraft, and that is enough to reconstruct what it was looking at.

The first clue is the aircraft's heading. About twenty-six minutes into the recording, the crew is briefly startled by a large round object. They zoom in and start laughing: it is the Moon rising. By Sheaffer's calculation the Moon rose that afternoon at about 17:20 local time, at an azimuth of 75 degrees. Since the camera was then pointed about 5 degrees left of the nose, the aircraft was flying on a heading of roughly 80 degrees, toward the east.

The same detail also fixes the time of day. Press reports spoke of "the night" of March 5, but a moonrise inside the footage places the recording around sunset.

The second clue is the direction of the lights. During the main part of the encounter the camera reads an azimuth of about −134 degrees relative to the aircraft, behind it and to the left, roughly the seven o'clock position. The lights sat on the horizon, within a degree or two. Plotted on a map with the aircraft's position and heading, that line points straight at the largest complex of oil platforms in the Bay of Campeche.

Cantarell: a forest of flares at sea

The Bay of Campeche is the heart of Mexico's oil industry. It is home to Cantarell, the country's largest oil field, about 80 kilometers offshore. Sheaffer counts more than 200 wells on nine platforms, many of them near Ciudad del Carmen. On the recording, one of the crew's voices places the lights right "at Carmen."

Those platforms burn off surplus gas in flares that run continuously. To a camera that turns heat into light, a gas flare tens of kilometers away is exactly a blazing point on a dark background, which is what the video shows.

By May 20, several skeptical analysts had already pointed to the oil flares as the likely source. On May 26 the same conclusion came, independently, from someone outside the skeptical camp: Captain Alejandro Franz, a former pilot who had flown extensively over the region and a member of the Mexican UFO research organization Alcione. Franz described the lights as steady flames from platforms between 50 and 90 kilometers from Ciudad del Carmen, and published photographs of the Cantarell flares for comparison with the video. Sheaffer corrects one detail of Franz's analysis, the aircraft's heading, but agrees on the substance.

The most telling comparison is inside the footage. At medium zoom, two brilliant "UFOs" appear behind some clouds. When the operator switches to the narrowest field of view, each one breaks up into individual flames: nine or more points that, according to Sheaffer, line up with daytime photographs of the platforms. The "eleven UFOs" were, in all likelihood, the flares of two oil complexes seen through hot, humid air at a coastline around sunset, which Miller called one of the most difficult atmospheric conditions for accurate imaging.

What about the radar?

The argument most often made for the case was that the aircraft's radar had also picked up objects. But according to Sheaffer's analysis, the position and number of the radar returns did not come close to matching the infrared lights: whatever the radar saw, it was not what the video shows. The lights were behind the aircraft; the radar targets were ahead of it.

The onboard radar data was not recorded, either, so what is known about it rests on the crew's recollection. The military radar operator in Ciudad del Carmen was contacted and was not showing any unknown objects.

Researcher Brad Sparks plotted the direction and range of the aircraft's radar returns on a map and found that some of them fell along Highway 186 on the Yucatán Peninsula. Their measured speed, about 52 knots, is consistent with trucks on that road. Sparks did not extend this to every return: his conclusion is that some of them, not all, are explained this way.

Campeche versus Nimitz

Campeche is often compared with the USS Nimitz encounter, also from 2004 and also known for a military infrared video. The comparison is useful mainly for what it shows Campeche lacks. In the Nimitz case, pilots said they saw the object with their own eyes, and several systems tracked it. In Campeche the lights existed only in the infrared, nobody saw them out of the window, and the radar was detecting things in a different direction.

What the two share is a lesson about sensors: an infrared video on its own gives neither range nor size. As Miller put it, the range of an object cannot be inferred from its brightness or size in the image unless the atmosphere and the sensor settings are known. That is why such systems often carry a laser rangefinder, and the one on the Merlin did not. A bright point can be something small and close or something enormous and far away, and the image cannot tell you which.

What remains open

The oil-flare explanation is strong. It rests on the data in the video itself, it was reached independently by a UFO researcher who knew the area, and it matches what appears when the zoom is closed. But the case leaves open questions, none of which involve aliens:

  • The data that was never released. SEDENA refused the UNAM researchers the technical flight data on the grounds that it was classified, while the video was being shown on television. Every later analysis has been built on what the screen shows, not on the aircraft's records.
  • The radar returns. The trucks explain some of them, by Sparks's own account, not all. And with no onboard radar recording, that can no longer be checked.
  • Why it was released that way. A defense secretary chose to make military footage public by giving it to a promoter of the paranormal rather than to a technical team. The general's explanation, that he saw no point in keeping it stored away, leaves open why he chose that recipient.

Campeche 2004 is remembered as the case of the Mexican Air Force's eleven UFOs. Read through the documents, it is something more useful: a near-textbook example of how a military sensor can turn something ordinary into a mystery, how hasty explanations from both sides muddied the debate, and how the answer was in the numbers in the corner of the screen all along.
