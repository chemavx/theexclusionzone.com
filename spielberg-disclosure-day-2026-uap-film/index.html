Spielberg's "Disclosure Day": Fifty Years of Alien Obsession, One Film, and a Director Who Now Believes

"Disclosure Day" opens in American theaters today. Fifty years after Close Encounters, Spielberg has moved from speculation to conviction — and built a film around authenticated government testimony, 222 declassified files, and a simple question: why haven't we been told?
Share
ChemaVX

Steven Spielberg believes aliens are already among us, and he's staking his filmmaking reputation on proving it through cinema. "Disclosure Day," released in American theaters today, is the product of fifty years of obsession, forty-two screenplay drafts, and a director who has moved from artistic speculation to declarative conviction.

The Filmmaker's Evolution From Skeptic to Believer

Spielberg directed "Close Encounters of the Third Kind" in 1977 as a post-Watergate meditation on government secrecy, consulting with Dr. J. Allen Hynek and Jacques Vallée — two of history's most rigorous UFO researchers. The film was imagined as "UFOs and Watergate," exploring distrust in institutions rather than celebrating contact with benevolent beings. For decades, Spielberg maintained public skepticism, stating he would need direct evidence before declaring extraterrestrial visitation real. That position shifted dramatically after the December 2017 New York Times exposé revealing the Pentagon's classified Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) and authenticated infrared footage of Navy encounters with unexplained objects.

The 2023 House Oversight Committee hearing proved the inflection point. When former Air Force intelligence officer David Grusch testified under oath about alleged government concealment of non-human craft recovery and reverse-engineering programs — alongside Navy pilots David Fravor and Ryan Graves describing their own encounters — Spielberg experienced what he describes as reinvigorated conviction. His shift from artistic speculation to stated belief accelerated through 2025 and into 2026. In recent interviews, he abandoned conditional language entirely: "I absolutely think that they have been here, and they are here." Grusch repeated those same demands on the steps of the Capitol just three days ago — our full investigation of that June 9 press conference is here.

How "Disclosure Day" Emerged From Childhood Wonder

Spielberg's fascination originates in a specific childhood memory. At age five or six, his father woke him from sleep and drove him to a New Jersey park to witness the Perseids meteor shower. That night crystallized his "love affair with the sky," leading to obsessive consumption of science fiction magazines — Galaxy, Analog, Amazing Stories — stacked high in the family bathroom. His teenage film "Firelight," shot in Arizona in 1964, centered on extraterrestrial visitors, establishing the thematic throughline connecting his entire seven-decade career.

During summer 2023, Spielberg spent two months writing a 50-60 page story treatment for what would become "Disclosure Day." He composed portions using the Notes app on his iPhone, then printed pages to read on his iPad. The treatment eventually reached screenwriter David Koepp via email, who would complete forty-two screenplay drafts — the most demanding collaboration of his career with Spielberg spanning five previous films including Jurassic Park and War of the Worlds.

The 2026 Political Context That Made Disclosure Timely

Former President Barack Obama's February 2026 podcast assertion that "aliens are real," followed by President Trump's directive to Pentagon agencies to release classified UAP files, created unprecedented mainstream momentum. Trump's executive order resulted in the May 8 release of over 160 declassified documents through the newly created PURSUE portal — including Apollo astronaut transcripts, military drone footage from Syria showing impossible acceleration, and accounts from Buzz Aldrin about observations during Apollo 11. A second tranche brought the total to 222 files. Our full analysis of what those 222 files reveal — and what they hide — is here.

While skeptical scientists noted many releases could be explained as optical artifacts or prosaic phenomena, the sheer volume of declassified material legitimized public curiosity in ways previous administrations had resisted. Spielberg recognized the cultural moment: "There's a 'critical mass' of people fascinated with whether we're alone in the universe. And if someone knows we're not alone, why haven't we been told?"

The Film's Structure: Whistleblower Thriller Meets Intimate Transformation

"Disclosure Day" premiered at Le Grand Rex in Paris on June 2, 2026, with the American theatrical release following today under Universal Pictures distribution. The narrative weaves parallel storylines that collide in revelation.

Josh O'Connor plays Daniel Kellner, a cybersecurity expert who steals forty-plus years of documentary evidence from Wardex — a shadowy corporation operating outside government oversight — containing classified files and technology from alien encounters dating to the 1947 Roswell incident. Wardex has been capturing, detaining, and experimentally studying extraterrestrial beings while reverse-engineering their technology for military application. Daniel's act of whistleblowing sets him on the run with his girlfriend Jane Blankenship (Eve Hewson), a former Catholic nun whose faith faces existential reckoning.

Simultaneously, Emily Blunt's Margaret Fairchild, a Kansas City television meteorologist, experiences her life upended when a cardinal makes eye contact with her. That contact activates dormant psychic abilities: she suddenly comprehends languages she never studied, reads the emotional states of strangers, and experiences visions of someone she's never met. During a live weather broadcast, alien linguistic clicks emerge from her mouth instead of forecast data — footage that immediately goes viral. Colman Domingo's Hugo Wakefield, another Wardex defector orchestrating the broader conspiracy exposure, recognizes Margaret's emergence as confirmation that she and Daniel share a connection extending back to childhood abductions they've suppressed.

Colin Firth portrays Noah Scanlon, Wardex's cold British-accented leader, wielding alien technology that allows him to telepathically locate targets and project his consciousness into others' minds. The device drives the film's psychological thriller tension — Jane becomes Scanlon's puppet, ordered to betray Daniel before her own will reasserts itself.

Production: Five Months Under Codename "Non-View"

Principal photography commenced February 26, 2025, across Atlanta, Huntington (Long Island), northern New Jersey, and New York City under the production codename "Non-View." Cinematographer Janusz Kamiński shot predominantly on 35mm film using Panavision cameras with anamorphic lenses, creating the classic 2.39:1 aspect ratio reminiscent of 1970s thrillers. Certain low-light sequences employed digital Sony CineAlta Venice 2 cameras.

For the sequence where Margaret speaks alien language on live television, Spielberg initially told Blunt the dialogue would receive AI post-processing enhancement. She rejected that approach entirely, utilizing her professional vocal training to generate authentic guttural clicking patterns herself — performing a continuous four-minute take without synthetic assistance. "I was terrified to use AI," she explained, wanting creative ownership of her character's otherworldly vocalization.

Composer John Williams' 30th Collaboration: Music That Doesn't Lead

John Williams composed his thirtieth score for a Spielberg film, markedly shifting his methodology from signature theme-driven symphonics. Discussing the score at SXSW, Spielberg revealed Williams' conscious aesthetic choice: "This time I'm going to write music not to lead the film, I'm going to write music under the film to give it the slight nudge forward." Rather than the sweeping melodic statements defining "Close Encounters" and "Indiana Jones," Williams provided subtle underscore allowing visuals and performances to occupy the foreground.

Critical Reception: Blunt's Performance Overshadows Mixed Assessments

"Disclosure Day" earned 81% positive reviews on Rotten Tomatoes (255 critics) with an average rating of 7.3/10. Metacritic assigned 74/100, indicating "generally favorable" reception. Emily Blunt's performance received near-universal superlatives. The Guardian's Peter Bradshaw awarded four stars, calling the film "never anything other than entertaining and grade-A fun" with "career-topper" work from Blunt. The Hollywood Reporter's David Rooney termed it "spellbinding" and "essential."

However, critical consensus identified structural inconsistencies. Owen Gleiberman of Variety noted Spielberg seemed "to be following rather than leading the decades of lore," failing to recapture the awe of "Close Encounters." The BBC's Nicholas Barber dismissed it as "a flimsy, outdated car-chase thriller with no ideas about aliens that we haven't heard before." The CGI aliens drew specific criticism, with the Los Angeles Times noting only "a nifty close-up of an eyeball" achieved visual distinction.

Thematic Resonance: Empathy, Secrecy, and Wonder

Spielberg has consistently framed "Disclosure Day" as addressing a question deeper than extraterrestrial confirmation: "Where has all our empathy gone?" Would disclosure of alien existence repair humanity's fractured divisions, or deepen them? Would religious communities experience existential crisis learning God's intelligence extends beyond Earth?

Colman Domingo summarized his character's urgency: "This 79-year terror campaign of obfuscation, lies and cover-up has to end!" — precisely 79 years since Roswell. Elizabeth Marvel's nun character offers the film's most philosophically resonant moment, asserting that extraterrestrial intelligence affirms rather than threatens faith, suggesting "God, like the universe he created, is far bigger and more complex than humans like to acknowledge."

Spielberg's Unambiguous Conviction

By May 2026, Spielberg had moved from diplomatic circumspection to declarative statement. His 2009 letter to Larry King stated he would "like to think" humanity wasn't alone. March 2026 SXSW remarks expressed "strong suspicion." May 2026 assertions invoked "overwhelming circumstantial evidence" justifying absolute conviction.

When asked directly if extraterrestrials have visited Earth, Spielberg responded: "Based on the circumstantial evidence of everything that I've gathered throughout my whole life, everybody I've listened to and every documentary I've ever watched and all the testimonies in Congress that I've heard, I absolutely think that they have been here, and they are here. And who knows, maybe they've always been here."

He acknowledged never personally witnessing a UFO, expressing humorous frustration: "With all the movies I made about non-human civilizations coming to Earth, I should be afforded the opportunity just to see one Tic Tac. I mean, just a Tic Tac would be fine by now!"

Fifty Years of Cinema Pointing at the Same Question

"Disclosure Day" arrives as capstone to fifty years of cinematic meditation on humanity's relationship with the cosmos. Whether audiences embrace or resist its vision of imminent revelation, the film testifies to a singular artist's conviction that the most profound unknown awaits not in distant galaxies, but in truths governments have already encountered — truths that, once universally known, might finally compel us toward empathy.

For a director who built a career imagining first contact, the most unsettling possibility is no longer that we are alone. It's that the answer has been sitting in a classified facility for seventy-nine years, and the only thing preventing disclosure has been the decision not to tell us.
