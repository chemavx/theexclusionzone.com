The Green Humanoid of Talavera: Spain's Most Unsettling Military UFO Encounter

On November 12, 1976, three soldiers guarding a Spanish air base opened fire on a three-meter luminous green humanoid. Fifty years later, the missing bullets, the classified file, and one soldier's unwavering testimony still define one of Spain's most unsettling military UFO encounters.

In the early hours of November 12, 1976, soldiers at a Spanish air base opened fire on a luminous green entity. Fifty years later, the missing bullets and classified records still haunt Spanish ufology.

Five soldiers in the predawn darkness witnessed something that altered military protocols

Around 1:45 a.m. on November 12, 1976, José María Trejo and Juan Carrizosa were completing their guard duty in the fuel storage zone of the Base Aérea de Talavera la Real, a military installation 15 kilometers south of Badajoz in Extremadura. The two men occupied separate guard posts approximately sixty meters apart when they first heard strange interference—metallic noises that within minutes transformed into an acute, piercing whistle that soldiers described as physically painful to the ears. The sound persisted for roughly five minutes before stopping abruptly. The silence lasted only briefly. A brilliant white light, comparable to a flare or bengal fire, illuminated the entire base for 15 to 20 seconds before darkness returned. Simultaneously, the base experienced partial blackouts in both electrical power and telephone communications.

When José Hidalgo arrived moments later with a military guard dog—a trained German shepherd—the three soldiers began a systematic patrol of the perimeter near the eucalyptus groves that bordered the installation. The animal's behavior immediately signaled something was wrong. Rather than displaying typical aggression toward an intruder, the dog became disoriented, returning to the soldiers in a confused, stumbling state after venturing into the eucalyptus thicket. Each time the handlers sent the dog forward, it returned in an identical condition, as though repelled by an invisible barrier.

The figure was approximately three meters tall and composed entirely of luminous green points of light

After releasing the dog multiple times without results, the soldiers heard branches cracking violently in the eucalyptus grove. The animal responded by adopting a defensive posture—circling the men in concentric patterns, a trained behavior meant to protect handlers from danger. Trejo suddenly felt a presence behind him. Turning, he encountered a sight that would define the remainder of his life: a humanoid form roughly three meters in height, entirely constructed from what witnesses described as small points of phosphorescent green light, more intensely bright along its edges. The head appeared small, possibly covered by a helmet-like structure. The arms were disproportionately long and extended outward in a cruciform position. The hands and feet were indistinct or absent entirely. The figure remained stationary, hovering slightly above the ground.

Trejo attempted to raise his Z-62 submachine gun to fire, but at that precise moment—as his finger moved toward the trigger—he experienced a sudden, total physical paralysis. His colleagues later reported him saying it felt like an invisible force piercing through his chest. He collapsed to the ground, losing consciousness momentarily. His two companions, witnessing both the entity and their partner's collapse, opened sustained fire with approximately 40 to 50 rounds between their two weapons, all directed at medium height toward the figure. According to eyewitness accounts, the entity's luminosity intensified dramatically at the moment of impact, producing a flash resembling a camera flash, before disappearing as if a television image fading to black. The distinctive high-pitched sound that had preceded the encounter returned briefly, then ceased entirely.

Fifty soldiers searched the zone the following morning and found no physical evidence the weapons had discharged

Military authorities mobilized approximately 50 to 60 soldiers in full formation to conduct a systematic search of the incident site at dawn. They discovered no ejected brass casings from the ammunition fired. No bullet impacts marked the concrete wall positioned directly behind where the entity had hovered. The weapons themselves had been test-fired by armory experts, confirming that ammunition had indeed been discharged. Yet the projectiles—roughly 40 to 50 of them—vanished without trace. The only unusual finding was a series of glass bottles, the glass itself appearing to have liquefied or melted, though no explanation for this phenomenon was documented in official reports.

Trejo's subsequent medical episodes across three separate hospitals were classified as "nervous adjustment difficulties"

Days following the incident, Trejo experienced sudden onset blindness while in the base dining facility. Military physicians transferred him initially to the Badajoz Hospital, where he remained for ten days without any diagnostic abnormalities being discovered through blood work, urinalysis, X-rays, or ophthalmological examination. Two days after discharge, while riding in a car with his girlfriend, vision loss recurred abruptly. He was then transferred to the Hospital del Aire, the Spanish Air Force's primary medical facility in Madrid. There Trejo remained for one month, undergoing extensive testing. Medical staff offered no specific diagnosis—only vague references to "nervous adjustment difficulties." During his hospitalization, he experienced an episode involving severe headaches beginning at the base of the skull, radiating to his forehead, culminating in complete blindness lasting approximately 15 minutes before gradually resolving. These episodes ceased entirely by early 1977. No hospital records of his treatment appear in any later declassified military documents.

The Spanish Defense Ministry maintained the entire case as classified military matter until public pressure forced limited disclosure

When researchers and UFO investigators began requesting information about the incident in the mid-1980s, the Defense Ministry responded with a standardized statement: all information regarding UFO sightings had been classified as "Reserved Matter" by the Joint Chiefs of Staff in 1979, citing Spain's Official Secrets Law (Ley 48/78). A judicial investigation was allegedly conducted immediately following the incident, with conclusions submitted to judicial authorities, yet no record of this investigation or its findings ever surfaced in official archives. When the Air Force Operations Command (MOA) took responsibility for OVNI (UFO) cases in 1992, no existing documentation regarding the Talavera incident appeared in transferred files. Multiple searches by base personnel and military archives yielded no documents corroborating the event's official investigation.

The case entered public consciousness through journalist J.J. Benítez's 1977 magazine article before military authorities could establish their narrative

The incident became known internationally through reporting by renowned Spanish journalist Juan José Benítez, who published an extensive account in the now-defunct magazine Mundo Desconocido (Unknown World) in November 1977, just eleven months after the occurrence. Benítez's article—titled "Three Spanish Soldiers Fire on an Extraterrestrial Tripulant"—presented Trejo's testimony in first-person narrative form and classified the entity as an actual spacecraft occupant. The article circulated among UFO research communities for decades before official documents became available. The timing of Benítez's publication, preceding official classification efforts by two years, ensured that civilian interpretations would compete with any institutional narrative the military might develop.

Badajoz province concentrated 35 percent of Spain's recorded UFO cases during the 1974–1980 period, yet Talavera remains its most militarily significant incident

Extremadura's historical density of UFO reports includes several documented military encounters. In June 1967, pilots of a T-33 aircraft en route to Talavera reported observing a bright, deformable object above the town of Montánchez, noting unusual radio interference during their approach. In January 1975, an air traffic controller at Talavera itself detected seven to eight moving radar echoes displaying velocity changes inconsistent with conventional aircraft, displaying both conventional airplane speeds and reactor jet speeds before the phenomena departed. These earlier incidents set a documented precedent for unexplained phenomena in the area, yet none approached the clarity of eyewitness testimony or the physical action (sustained rifle fire) that characterized the November 1976 encounter.

Declassification provided copies of newspaper articles and correspondence requests rather than original military investigation files

The Spanish Ministry of Defense declassified 80 UFO-related expedientes (official case files) totaling 1,900 pages in 2016, covering phenomena recorded between 1962 and 1995. The Talavera case file, designated Expediente 761112, contains primarily photocopies of the original Diario de Caracas (Caracas Daily) article reporting the incident, correspondence from researchers requesting information (dated 1984–1991), and official Defense responses explaining that information remained classified. The actual military investigation report, if one was ever formally completed, does not appear in the declassified material. Heavy redaction marks out names of officers and informants throughout the file. The official summary notes simply state that the case remains unresolved, though meteorological data and radar records from the night in question are absent.

Vicente-Juan Ballester, the leading Spanish skeptical ufologist, attributes the encounter to psychological suggestion rather than an extraterrestrial event, yet the military itself never reached a conclusion

Valencia-based researcher Vicente-Juan Ballester, who participated in Spain's OVNI declassification process during the 1990s, has repeatedly argued that the incident represents a collective hallucination reinforced by fear and confusion in a military context. Ballester notes that the base command never officially classified the event as a genuine UFO encounter; rather, the expediente was created solely because researchers and civilians submitted written inquiries demanding information. The absence of any physical evidence—no melted glass fragments retained for analysis, no photographic documentation, no electromagnetic interference recordings—supports a psychological explanation in Ballester's assessment. However, even Ballester acknowledges the deliberate compartmentalization of the case and the military's decision to prevent the soldiers from communicating with one another for decades. Skeptical analysis does not explain why institutional secrecy would be necessary if merely a mass panic episode had occurred.

José María Trejo, the sole surviving principal witness in his nineties, maintains complete consistency in his account across five decades of interviews and maintains the entity saved his life by preventing him from absorbing gunfire

Trejo, now retired from military service and working as a guitar instructor, has granted dozens of interviews since the 1990s without altering any significant detail of his account. He consistently describes the entity's apparent awareness of his intention to fire his weapon, manifesting as a physical force that rendered him incapacitated before he could discharge his rounds. According to Trejo's interpretation, this intervention positioned him outside the line of fire from his companions' weapons—fire that could easily have been lethal had he remained standing. His medical episodes in the months following the incident remain medically unexplained. The military's decision to restrict communication between the soldiers for 25 years, followed by his freedom to discuss the event publicly after that period, suggests institutional concern about narrative consistency rather than concern over fabrication. Trejo's unwavering testimony across nearly 50 years, without embellishment or modification, distinguishes his account from typical misremembered or mythologized events.

The case remains one of Spain's most rigorously documented military UFO encounters despite—or because of—the absence of conclusive explanation

Half a century after the November 12, 1976 incident, Talavera la Real continues to represent the intersection of institutional credibility and genuine mystery. The presence of armed military personnel, the firing of actual weapons, the official investigation (however incompletely documented), and the declassification of related files grant this case a legitimacy that purely civilian UFO reports lack. Yet the absence of physical remains, the loss of medical records, the redaction of investigative documents, and the official silence maintained across four decades prevent any definitive resolution. The case exists in perpetual historiographical limbo—too well-documented to dismiss, yet too incompletely explained to confirm. For Spanish ufology, Talavera remains a boundary marker: the point where military institution and unexplained phenomenon collide, leaving only questions.
