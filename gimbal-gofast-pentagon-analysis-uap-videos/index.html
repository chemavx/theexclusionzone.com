GIMBAL and GOFAST: What the Pentagon's Own Analysis Reveals — and What It Deliberately Withholds

The Pentagon released GIMBAL and GOFAST in 2020. Five years of formal analysis later, both remain officially unidentified — and the synchronized radar data, full telemetry, and complete video that would resolve them has never been declassified.

ChemaVX

The Pentagon officially released three infrared Navy videos in April 2020 depicting unidentified aerial phenomena. Five years later, after formal government analysis, two of the three cases remain officially unresolved — and the data that would settle the debate has never been made public.

The Three Videos and Their Origin

The Pentagon authorized public release of three infrared targeting pod videos on April 27, 2020, ending years of unauthorized circulation. One video, designated FLIR1 or "Tic Tac," originated from a November 14, 2004 encounter involving the USS Nimitz Carrier Strike Group off the California coast — documented in full in our investigation of the Nimitz incident. Two additional videos — GIMBAL and GOFAST — came from January 2015 operations by the USS Theodore Roosevelt strike group off the East Coast, recorded using the AN/ASQ-228 ATFLIR pod.

The Pentagon stated the release "does not reveal any sensitive capabilities or systems" and was intended to "clear up any misconceptions by the public on whether or not the footage that has been circulating was real." The videos' authenticity was confirmed by the Navy in September 2019. The official characterization of what they depict remained deliberately vague: "The aerial phenomena observed in the videos remain characterized as 'unidentified.'"

GOFAST: The Parallax Explanation That Divides Experts

The GOFAST video shows a small infrared hotspot appearing to skim the ocean surface at extraordinary speed. When the ATFLIR pod locked onto the target, a crew member exclaimed "Whoa! Got him!" — an exclamation that became iconic in UAP circles. The footage includes on-screen data critical to later analysis: sensor azimuth, elevation, field of view, aircraft altitude and speed, and pod range to target.

In February 2025, AARO released a formal case-resolution analysis concluding the object exhibited no anomalous performance. The agency extracted range and angular measurements from the display, reconstructed plausible aircraft headings, incorporated historical wind data from 13,000 feet altitude, and computed a family of kinematic solutions. Their conclusion: "AARO assesses with high confidence that the object did not move at anomalous speeds."

AARO attributed the apparent extreme velocity to motion parallax — a well-documented optical effect where a moving observer perceives stationary or slow-moving distant objects as traveling much faster. The agency estimated the object's actual speed between 5 mph and 92 mph depending on wind conditions, with the object "moving with the wind" at most times. The apparent low altitude over water, AARO argued, resulted from the jet's own motion creating a perspective illusion.

Pilots directly involved rejected this explanation. They emphasized that GOFAST occurred during a multi-week period when USS Theodore Roosevelt aviators reported persistent radar tracks of multiple objects — sometimes dozens simultaneously. Some radar targets held fixed positions despite winds exceeding 120 knots, behavior inconsistent with any object passively drifting with wind. Fighter pilot Ryan Graves has consistently stated that treating GOFAST as an isolated visual anomaly ignores the broader operational context of coordinated multi-object contacts.

The Navy has never officially endorsed AARO's parallax model as definitive. The Department of Defense continues to classify GOFAST as "Unidentified" in its official categorization. Critically, AARO based its analysis on a compressed version of the video — the original file and complete metadata were lost, constraining the analysis's precision.

GIMBAL: Rotation Artifact or Physical Maneuver?

The GIMBAL video shows a bright, roughly oval heat signature moving against a cloud backdrop. At approximately 22 seconds in, the object's image visibly rotates, prompting one pilot to remark "It's rotating." During the encounter, pilots on intercom mention "a whole fleet of them," indicating multiple objects visible on situational awareness displays simultaneously.

Skeptical analyst Mick West proposed that the rotation results not from the object physically rolling but from optical artifacts within the ATFLIR system. The pod uses a multi-axis gimbal mechanism with internal image derotation designed to keep the horizon stable as the pod's optics rotate. Bright infrared sources like hot jet exhaust can produce asymmetric glare around the target image. As the gimbal system compensates and derotates the image, the glare pattern can appear to rotate relative to the stabilized horizon even if the underlying object remains stationary.

Competing technical analyses challenge this artifact-only explanation. When researchers examine stabilized footage, the object does not rotate in synchronization with background cloud structures and horizon references — a decoupling suggesting the rotation is intrinsic to the object rather than a sensor artifact. Frame-by-frame analysis also indicates the FLIR system's downward pan as it maintains track suggests a nearby object being approached by the recording aircraft, not a distant object moving away.

AARO has never published a formal technical resolution for GIMBAL equivalent to its GOFAST analysis. The official AARO website lists GIMBAL under "unresolved cases." The data that would settle the debate — full-length video, synchronized radar tracks from the carrier's E-2D Hawkeye, complete telemetry and pod metadata — has never been released to the public.

FLIR1: Authentic Footage, No Resolution Data

The FLIR1 video contains no embedded range solution in the public release. Without range data, neither size nor absolute speed can be computed from the video alone. Commander David Fravor described the object as exhibiting acceleration "like nothing I've ever seen," but the video alone cannot resolve the physics without corroborating multi-sensor data. The provenance is solid — authentic Navy capture — but the kinematic questions remain open. Neither the DOD nor the Navy has published a formal analytic conclusion about the object itself.

The Operational Context That Changes Everything

A crucial element largely absent from public discussion involves the full operational context. During January-March 2015, USS Theodore Roosevelt aviators reported persistent radar contacts — not isolated sightings, but recurring patterns of multiple tracks sometimes numbering in the dozens. Some radar targets maintained stationary positions despite wind speeds exceeding 120 knots. Others exhibited maneuvering behavior across weeks, not hours.

In congressional testimony in 2023, Graves and other aviators consistently referenced the multi-object radar environment and coordination among contacts — testimony that directly echoes the whistleblower accounts about Immaculate Constellation's systematic UAP tracking described in our investigation of the November 2024 hearing. The public has never been provided with synchronized radar data, E-2D Hawkeye tracks, or full telemetry logs from these encounters.

AARO's Technical Framework for Genuine UAP

To understand what the Pentagon considers genuinely anomalous, AARO published specific technical parameters in 2023. Objects meeting AARO's "target package" for genuine UAP typically exhibit round shapes, 1-4 meters in diameter, white, silver, or translucent coloration, performance altitudes of 10,000 to 30,000 feet, and velocities ranging from stationary hover to Mach 2. Despite such performance, no thermal exhaust is associated with these objects. Additional signatures include intermittent X-Band radar returns and radio frequency emissions in the 1-3 GHz and 8-12 GHz ranges.

The specificity of these attributes suggests AARO possesses higher-quality source data beyond the compressed, metadata-limited videos released to the public — data from which the agency derived this technical target package. If such data exists in classified holdings, the three released videos fall significantly short of the best available evidence.

What Remains Classified

The Pentagon acknowledged in 2022 that additional UAP footage existed beyond the three released videos but declined to release more, citing national security concerns. Former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense Christopher Mellon, who facilitated the initial release of the three videos to The New York Times in 2017, has suggested higher-quality multi-sensor packages exist in government holdings. Multiple Navy pilots interviewed by Popular Mechanics stated they had viewed a longer, clearer version of the 2004 Nimitz encounter — reportedly 8 to 10 minutes long and significantly clearer than the 34-second FLIR1 clip released publicly.

The same asymmetry runs through the USS Russell footage of 2019: the leaked night-vision clips drew years of argument over a pyramid shape that turned out to be the camera's own aperture, while the two weeks of swarm contacts off the Channel Islands that produced them were never explained.

The three PURSUE releases completed through June 2026 — totaling 294 declassified files — have added new UAP footage but have not released the multi-sensor packages from the 2004 or 2015 encounters. Our investigations of the first 222 files and the third PURSUE release cover what has been disclosed — and what continues to be withheld. David Grusch's testimony before Congress, examined in our June 2026 investigation, describes a pattern of classified data withheld from oversight bodies that aligns directly with the gap between what AARO claims to have analyzed and what it has released publicly.

Official Status: Unidentified, Not Resolved

The Pentagon maintains a crucial distinction: the three Navy videos remain officially categorized as "unidentified" by the Department of Defense, even after AARO's technical analysis of GOFAST. "Unidentified" does not mean "anomalous" or "extraordinary" — it means only that identification has not been achieved. AARO may have provided plausible explanations based on available evidence, but the Navy and DoD have not formally adopted those explanations as definitive resolutions.

Pilots insist they witnessed coordinated multi-object behavior that no single parallax calculation or glare-artifact model adequately addresses. Skeptics argue that absent direct evidence of anomalous performance, conventional explanations remain preferable. Government agencies simultaneously state they have found no evidence of extraterrestrial origin while refusing to fully declassify data that might definitively resolve the question either way. The Pentagon appears content allowing this stalemate to persist.

Five Years After Declassification, the Questions Remain

The GIMBAL and GOFAST videos represent a watershed moment in U.S. government transparency regarding UAP — officially acknowledging encounters and providing infrared documentation. Yet five years after formal declassification, fundamental questions remain unresolved. AARO's parallax modeling for GOFAST provides a mathematically consistent explanation for apparent high speed but does not incorporate the multi-object radar context that pilot testimony emphasizes. GIMBAL's rotation remains unexplained in any official public analysis. FLIR1 lacks the range data needed for kinematic resolution.

Definitive progress requires release of synchronized multi-sensor packages — radar tracks, complete telemetry, atmospheric data, full-length video — or official acknowledgment that such data exists in classified holdings and an explanation of why national security requires its continued concealment. After 294 declassified files and three rounds of PURSUE releases, neither has been provided.
