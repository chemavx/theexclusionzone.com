The Wilson-Davis Memo: Inside a Pentagon Official's Blocked Access to America's UFO Reverse Engineering Program

A high-ranking Pentagon intelligence officer was denied access to a classified reverse engineering program despite holding statutory authority to oversee it.
Share
ChemaVX

A high-ranking Pentagon intelligence officer claimed he was explicitly denied access to a classified program handling recovered non-human spacecraft—despite holding, by his own account, the statutory authority to oversee it. That extraordinary claim sits at the heart of leaked notes describing a 2002 meeting between Admiral Thomas Wilson and astrophysicist Dr. Eric Davis, documents that later became part of the official Congressional Record. It is a remarkable story—and one that Wilson himself flatly denies ever happened.

How the Story Emerged

The notes describe a conversation said to have taken place on October 16, 2002, at an EG&G Special Projects facility in Las Vegas. Davis—then a consultant working in defense and aerospace research—is said to have documented the discussion in detailed notes that remained obscure for years. They surfaced publicly around 2018, after being discovered among the personal archive of Apollo 14 astronaut Edgar Mitchell, who had died in 2016. Mitchell had reportedly received a copy through his involvement with NIDS (the National Institute for Discovery Science), a privately funded research organization investigating unexplained phenomena.

The document's path to the public record was a winding one. Copies circulated online—including on Reddit—before the notes achieved a kind of official status when they were entered into Congressional filings accessible through congress.gov. The version now in the archive runs to roughly 19 pages.

The backstory traces to an April 1997 Pentagon briefing, where UFO researcher Steven Greer, retired Navy Commander Will Miller, and Edgar Mitchell presented material on alleged UAP-related programs to senior military officials. According to the notes, that meeting is what set Wilson's subsequent inquiry in motion.

Wilson's Reported Search

Wilson—at the time Deputy Director of the Defense Intelligence Agency and serving in a senior Joint Staff intelligence role—is described in the notes as spending several weeks searching for an alleged UFO recovery program after that 1997 briefing. The account has him consulting senior acquisition officials and being directed toward special-projects records within the office overseeing defense acquisition and technology.

According to the notes, Wilson found an unusual records compartment containing "index abstracts" of programs. He is said to have called several program managers; most told him they weren't what he was looking for, while others pointed him toward the same small operation—managers working in separate compartments within the same records group, an arrangement the account presents as typical of deeply concealed "black" programs.

The program was reportedly run by three individuals: a security director, a program director, and a corporate attorney—described in the notes as calling themselves a "gatekeeper" committee. The contractor was characterized only as one of the top U.S. aerospace technology firms; the notes say Wilson declined to name it, citing "core secrets." Over the years, speculation has pointed to various major defense contractors, though none of this is established.

The Committee's Reported Refusal

When Wilson allegedly demanded a formal briefing, invoking his oversight authority, the managers are said to have initially resisted before arranging an in-person meeting at a secure facility. There, according to the notes, they explained their unusual structure: an earlier audit had nearly exposed the program, and they had since negotiated an arrangement with a special-access oversight body that left access criteria in the hands of the contractor committee itself.

The notes claim the managers refused to disclose what those criteria were, telling Wilson he lacked the "need to know" despite his clearances and position. The access roster—the list of personnel cleared into the program—is described as containing several hundred individuals, overwhelmingly civilian scientists, engineers, and managers. Strikingly, the account says it included no president, no members of Congress, and only a handful of Pentagon names—an absence the notes present as evidence that elected oversight had been bypassed entirely.

The Central Claim

The most dramatic passage describes the moment Wilson allegedly pressed for details. The program, the managers supposedly revealed, was a reverse-engineering operation for recovered technology not of human origin. When Wilson reportedly assumed they meant Soviet or Chinese hardware, the account has them rejecting that emphatically: they claimed to possess an intact craft, and that—in the words attributed to them in the notes—it was "not made by man."

The program had supposedly been operating for years with very slow progress, which the managers attributed to deliberate isolation: prohibited from seeking outside expertise or collaborating with the wider scientific community, their compartmentalized workforce advanced, in the words of the notes, "agonizingly slow."

When Wilson reportedly asked about specific historical cases—Roswell, an alleged Holloman Air Force Base landing, MJ-12, and the Socorro and Bentwaters incidents—the managers are said to have fallen silent and declined to discuss them.

Threats and Retaliation

According to the notes, Wilson's complaint to a senior special-access oversight review group backfired. He was allegedly warned that if he persisted, he would lose his expected promotion to Director of DIA and face forced early retirement—and told to drop the matter, on the grounds that it "didn't fall within [his] oversight." The chairman who is said to have delivered this warning is not reliably identified, and the notes themselves do not establish his identity beyond his role.

Wilson, the account claims, was furious but ultimately backed down. Months later, in early 1998, he reportedly raised the subject again with a newly appointed acquisition undersecretary, who is quoted offering a cryptic line—that UFOs are real but alleged abductions are not—before again advising him to let it go.

What the Notes Describe—and What They Don't

Taken at face value, the Wilson-Davis notes describe a profound breakdown in accountability: a senior intelligence official told by contractors that his statutory authority simply did not apply to their program. That inversion of the normal chain of command is what makes the document so striking to UAP researchers—and so contested.

The notes touch on a genuine and documented feature of the U.S. national-security system: Special Access Programs, the most tightly compartmented category of classified work, are real, and questions about their oversight and budgetary opacity have been raised by credible figures for decades. The notes use that real backdrop to frame an extraordinary claim—that one such program concealed non-human technology. The first part is a documented institutional concern; the second is an unverified assertion resting entirely on the notes themselves.

It is worth sitting with the internal logic of the account. If the program truly possessed non-human technology and hundreds of cleared specialists, its admitted "agonizingly slow" progress is curious—explained in the notes by self-imposed isolation from the peer review and collaboration that drives real scientific advance. Whether that reads as a plausible detail or a narrative convenience is left to the reader.

The Authentication Problem

Here the story becomes genuinely difficult, because its central figure rejects it outright. In statements to investigative journalist Billy Cox, Admiral Wilson called the notes "fiction." He said he wouldn't recognize Eric Davis, disputed specific details—pointing out, for instance, that descriptions of his Navy uniform made no sense given his status at the time—and insisted he had not been in Las Vegas in the period described. By his account, the entire document is a fabrication.

Against that flat denial sit several partial corroborations. Steven Greer, present at the 1997 Pentagon briefing that the notes credit as the trigger, has said the substance of the account is "substantially correct with only minor errors," while declining to specify which details he meant. Christopher Mellon, a former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Intelligence under two administrations, has stated his understanding that Davis authored the notes—an attribution from someone with real national-security credentials. Eric Davis himself has neither confirmed nor denied authorship, citing his clearances.

So the document's status is irreducibly ambiguous. People with credible backgrounds have lent weight to its authenticity as a record of a conversation; the named protagonist insists the conversation never happened; and even those who defend it concede unspecified errors. None of this resolves the deeper question of whether anything the notes describe is true.

The same gap between a documented event and its claimed contents runs through the case of Gary McKinnon, the Scottish systems administrator who got inside 97 US military and NASA computers between 2001 and 2002 using default passwords. The intrusion is on the record; what he says he saw there is testimony, and he has no copy of any of it.

Coherent, Consistent, and Impossible to Verify

The Wilson-Davis notes present a coherent, internally consistent account of institutional capture—a contractor-run black program said to have resisted oversight by the Deputy Director of Defense Intelligence. What makes the document compelling is also what makes it impossible to verify: it is a secondhand record of a private conversation, partly corroborated by interested parties, and flatly denied by the man at its center. Whether the recovered object it describes was extraterrestrial, misidentified classified hardware, or never existed at all cannot be settled from the notes themselves.

What the document does capture—regardless of its ultimate authenticity—is a genuine and recurring anxiety about the modern secrecy system: the worry that some compartmented programs may have drifted beyond the reach of the people constitutionally meant to oversee them. That concern long predates these notes, and it outlives the question of whether this particular meeting ever took place. The reader is left, as so often in this field, with a story that is either one of the most significant disclosures of its kind—or a carefully constructed fiction. The evidence, as it stands, does not allow us to be certain which.
