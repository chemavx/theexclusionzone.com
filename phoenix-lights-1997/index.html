Phoenix Lights 1997: When Thousands Witnessed Something Extraordinary in the Arizona Sky

Tens of thousands of witnesses, a governor who later reversed his story, and two separate events in one night. The complete investigation of the 1997 Phoenix Lights.
Share
ChemaVX

On March 13, 1997, between 7:30 and 10:30 p.m., tens of thousands of people across Arizona and southern Nevada watched in silence as massive lights moved across the night sky. What unfolded that evening became one of the most credible mass UFO sightings in modern history—not because of blurry footage or isolated witnesses, but because entire families, police officers, pilots, and a sitting state governor all saw the same impossible thing.

Two Separate Sightings on One March Night

The events of March 13, 1997 actually consisted of two separate but closely timed sightings that continue to confound researchers nearly three decades later. The first incident began around 7:55 p.m. when a witness in Henderson, Nevada reported a large V-shaped object traveling southeast. Within minutes, a former police officer in Paulden, Arizona near Prescott witnessed a cluster of reddish-orange lights disappearing over the horizon. What followed was an unprecedented wave of telephoned reports flooding into the National UFO Reporting Center, law enforcement offices, local news media, and Luke Air Force Base to the west of Phoenix.

The geographic scope proved staggering. Reports poured in from Chino Valley, Prescott, Prescott Valley, Dewey, Cordes Junction, Wickenburg, Cavecreek, and dozens of communities north and west of Phoenix. By 8:15 p.m., a coordinated formation of lights was visible approaching from the north and moving south across the state at a steady, deliberate pace. The object reportedly traveled approximately 300 miles across Arizona and into the Tucson area—a journey that would have taken roughly two hours at the observed speed.

The Ley Family Watches It Pass Overhead

The first event's most compelling aspect emerged from detailed witness testimony. A family north of Phoenix—Tim Ley, his wife Bobbi, his son Hal, and grandson Damien Turnidge—observed the lights transform from five distinct points into an upside-down V shape as they approached. When the formation passed directly overhead at approximately 100 to 150 feet altitude, witnesses described a structure resembling a massive carpenter's square or sergeant's stripes, with five distinct lights embedded into its underside. The object moved slowly enough to appear hovering, traveling in complete silence despite its enormous apparent size.

Actor and amateur pilot Kurt Russell was airborne in his private plane that evening and reported the lights to air traffic control. His pilot's perspective added credibility—he described seeing six objects in formation, a detail that emerged from his later interview on the subject. Russell's participation proved significant because it came from someone trained to identify aircraft and who was above the event, able to observe from an altitude that ruled out certain terrestrial explanations.

Between 8:30 and 8:45 p.m., witnesses in Glendale, a Phoenix suburb, reported the formation passing overhead at high altitude before disappearing into thin cloud cover. Amateur astronomer Mitch Stanley in Scottsdale observed the same formation through a telescope and identified the lights as aircraft — the single most consequential piece of testimony in the case, and the subject of its own section below.

A second, entirely separate event unfolded approximately two hours later, around 10:00 p.m., over the Sierra Estrella mountains southwest of Phoenix. This time, witnesses reported a row of brilliant, near-stationary lights hanging in the sky. The stationary aspect proved key—these lights appeared to hover, descend slowly, and eventually wink out behind the mountain range. Numerous photographs and videos captured this second event, creating visual documentation that would dominate subsequent discussions of the incident.

The sheer number of credible witnesses proved extraordinary. The National UFO Reporting Center received reports from hundreds of independent witnesses across multiple counties and communities. Many were well-positioned professionals: former airline pilots with thousands of flight hours, former law enforcement officers, families gathered on their lawns, drivers pulled over to observe.

The Man Who Looked Through a Telescope

Of the thousands of people who looked up that night, one looked up at sixty times magnification — and his account has been the most consequential and the most misused piece of testimony in the case ever since.

Mitch Stanley, an amateur astronomer, was in the yard of his Scottsdale home with his mother when she pointed out the lights crossing the sky. He swung a large Dobsonian telescope with a ten-inch mirror onto the formation, tracked it for about a minute, and gave her a one-word answer: planes.

Tony Ortega's cover investigation for Phoenix New Times, published on the first anniversary of the sighting, quotes him without hedging: "They were planes. There's no way I could have mistaken that." Through the eyepiece he did not see a solid structure with lights set into it. He saw separate lights belonging to separate aircraft flying in formation.

What happened to that testimony is the uncomfortable part. Stanley told his mother that night. The next day a fellow amateur astronomer, Jack Jones, telephoned the Arizona Republic and city councilwoman Frances Barwood to pass it along. Neither returned his call. The one witness who had used optics stayed out of the public record for more than a year.

And here is the turn that almost nobody reports, including the people who cite Stanley as the end of the argument: identifying the formation as aircraft does not identify the aircraft. A spokesman for the Maryland Air National Guard told New Times that its A-10s — which have squarish wings — never flew north of Phoenix that night, which rules them out as the planes Stanley watched at 8:15 p.m.

So the skeptical account and the believer's account are both incomplete in the same place. Twenty-nine years on, the only man who put a telescope on the first event said "planes," and no one has ever said whose.

How the Governor's Press Conference Backfired

The incident's historical importance lies not primarily in what occurred, but in how credible institutions and officials responded to public panic. Arizona Governor Fife Symington III faced immediate pressure to address what thousands of his constituents had witnessed. Instead of taking the reports seriously, Symington held a press conference three months later, on June 19, where he displayed his chief of staff dressed in a rubber alien costume, claiming he had "found the culprit." He laughed with attendees while the state's residents felt mocked and gaslit.

This response achieved precisely one thing: it established official cover for whatever had occurred. The flare explanation hardened into the official account over the months that followed; an Associated Press dispatch dated July 26, 1997 attributed it to Drew Sullins, a spokesman for the Maryland Air National Guard. The explanation satisfied skeptics and gave officials plausible deniability. The public narrative collapsed into competing theories: either the entire state was mistaken about what it saw, or military personnel were conducting unannounced exercises over populated areas.

Symington's behavior proved particularly consequential because he was not a random official—he had been a military pilot with extensive experience identifying aircraft. Ten years after the incident, in 2007, Symington would publicly reverse his position, stating that he had personally witnessed "a massive delta-shaped craft" that "silently navigate[d] over Squaw Peak" and that he believed it was "otherworldly."

June 19, 1997: The Morning and the Afternoon

The alien-costume press conference is routinely placed within days of the sighting. It was held on Thursday, June 19, 1997 — three months later. And what makes it the strangest document in the case is not the joke. It is what the governor had said that same morning.

Earlier that day, Symington announced on television that he would order the Arizona Department of Public Safety to look into the lights, promising "all the necessary inquiries. We're going to get to the bottom of this. We're going to find out if it was a UFO."

Hours later he stood at a lectern in the state capitol and pulled a mask off his chief of staff. The caption Associated Press distributed with Scott Troyanos's photograph that day puts both halves in a single sentence:

"Arizona Gov. Fife Symington pulls a mask off of aide Jay Heiler, dressed up as an alien, during a late afternoon news conference at the state capitol in downtown Phoenix Thursday June 19, 1997. Symington, earlier in the day, said he would order the Arizona Department of Public Safety to look into the mysterious lights seen over Phoenix, but later insisted he was joking."

He promised to get to the bottom of it in the morning and turned it into a sketch by the afternoon. Both halves carry the same date and are documented separately.

What Symington Wrote Ten Years Later, Under His Own Byline

On November 9, 2007, out of office, Symington published a commentary on CNN headlined "Symington: I saw a UFO in the Arizona sky." This is not a documentary soundbite or a quote filtered through a third party. It is his own text, with his name and a date on it, and it says considerably more than the fragments usually lifted from it:

"In 1997, during my second term as governor of Arizona, I saw something that defied logic and challenged my reality. I witnessed a massive delta-shaped, craft silently navigate over Squaw Peak, a mountain range in Phoenix, Arizona. It was truly breathtaking. (…) As a pilot and a former Air Force Officer, I can definitively say that this craft did not resemble any man-made object I'd ever seen. And it was certainly not high-altitude flares because flares don't fly in formation."

Three things in that commentary deserve emphasis, and none of them is the part that gets repeated.

First, he puts the witness count at "hundreds — if not thousands." The exact phrase is "witnessed by hundreds -- if not thousands -- of people in Arizona." That is far below the figures that circulate in accounts of the case, and it comes from the witness with the greatest interest in the number being large. When the source most motivated to inflate a figure settles on "hundreds," that is the figure to print.

Second, he explains the press conference, and his explanation is not the one in circulation. He writes: "I decided to lighten the mood of the state by calling a press conference where my chief of staff arrived in an alien costume," and adds, "I would now like to set the record straight. I never meant to ridicule anyone." By his account it was his own decision, taken to defuse public alarm. Nothing in this text — and nothing in any document we have been able to verify — supports the widely repeated claim that the ridicule was staged at the direction of military or federal officials.

Third, and this is the part that actually sustains the case: "My office did make inquiries as to the origin of the craft, but to this day they remain unanswered." The office of the governor of Arizona asked where the object had come from and, a decade later, had still not been told.

"Nothing Unusual on Radar" Does Not Mean "No Returns"

It is the most quoted line in the case and the most badly read. The Federal Aviation Administration found nothing unusual in its traffic data and radar at Luke Air Force Base recorded no anomalous track. From there the account has leapt, for years, to the claim that the object was invisible to radar.

The Phoenix New Times investigation recorded what the officials themselves actually said. Air traffic controller Bill Grava, on duty at Sky Harbor that night, distinguished the airport's local coverage from higher-altitude traffic — which is worked out of Albuquerque — and from the distant North Tac range. Luke spokeswoman Stacey Cotton explained that a high formation outside restricted airspace, or a drop inside the gunnery range, would not necessarily be unusual.

In other words: nobody said there were no returns. They said they saw nothing that, by their own operational standards, was worth writing down. That does not identify the 8:15 p.m. formation. It also does not license the claim that it was undetectable.

Too Many Witnesses, Too Little Evidence

The Phoenix Lights of 1997 represent a watershed moment where the credibility of mass public perception collided head-on with institutional power. The incident involved too many witnesses across too wide a geographic area to dismiss as collective hallucination, yet too few material artifacts to definitively prove what occurred. What remains undisputed is that something unusual moved across Arizona's night sky that evening, that thousands of sober, responsible people witnessed it, and that official channels responded not with investigation but with ridicule—a pattern that continues to raise questions about what governments know regarding unidentified phenomena. Numbers alone never settled anything: more than sixty schoolchildren in Zimbabwe gave consistent accounts in 1994 and that case remains exactly as open.
