Roswell already has its legend, told a thousand times: the ranch, the debris, the press release that said "flying disc" and the retraction the next day. We have covered that story. What matters now is something else: why, seventy-eight years later, the central questions are still unanswered — and what has changed recently that reopens the case.

A video that turned up quietly

In September 2025, a 21-minute file titled "The Roswell Incident" appeared without announcement in the US National Archives, catalogued as 341-ROSWELL-41. No press conference. It was simply there for anyone who knew to look.

What circulated on social media that September was this: a crater in the desert, scattered metallic fragments, and a dark silhouette among the debris that spread as "the alien body from the Roswell crash."

It is not that, and we can say so with measurements on the table. In July 2026 we put the original tape through a full forensic analysis — frame extraction, automatic cut detection, Error Level Analysis, per-segment noise signatures, compression structure and perceptual hashing of key frames — and the result leaves no room: the crater sequence is not footage of any location. It is a printed photograph, filmed on a copy stand. The affine registration between the first and last frames of the sequence gives a 2.1% scale change and a 46-pixel vertical drift: the signature of a camera panning across a still page, not of a camera moving over terrain. Immediately afterwards a page turns, and the final shot holds for nineteen seconds on the full-page plate. The "silhouette" shares exactly the grain of the photograph around it — noise σ 7.78 inside the shape against 7.65 outside, a ratio of 1.02 — and its centre does not shift. It is printed there. Nobody inserted anything.

The coordinates that circulated alongside the video therefore come from no location footage at all. And the twenty-one minutes have a perfectly identifiable origin: fourteen minutes of studio filming of the Air Force report itself, interview transcripts and the books that built the Roswell canon, plus seven minutes of archive film of high-altitude balloon operations. It is, almost piece by piece, the supporting material for the 1997 Case Closed report. The uncomfortable question is not where the tape came from, which is known. It is why it was circulated as something it was not.

The material that should not exist

The official explanation since 1994 points to Project Mogul: high-altitude balloons carrying radar reflectors, designed to detect Soviet nuclear tests. Charles B. Moore, the physicist who designed those balloons, confirmed that the materials found at the ranch — foil, balsa sticks, adhesive tape — matched his description.

The problem is what Major Jesse Marcel described. Marcel, the intelligence officer who examined the debris personally, insisted until his death that the material could not be cut, burned or bent — not even with a hammer. He described a metal with plastic properties. A Mogul balloon hitting the ground would have come apart into brittle fragments of paper, rubber and wood. Not into something indestructible.

Marcel also mentioned sticks carrying symbols that looked like hieroglyphs. The official explanation: adhesive tape with floral motifs from a New York toy company. The question nobody has answered satisfactorily is how a trained intelligence officer mistakes a decorative floral pattern for symbolic writing.

The diary nobody has finished analysing

Marcel's family recently revealed the existence of his personal 1947 diary, still held by his grandchildren. In the pages covering August of that year there is a notable change in handwriting — something Ben Smith, a former CIA officer who has examined the document using intelligence analysis techniques, reads as possible signs of deliberate encoding or of writing under pressure.

This is a document contemporary with the events, not testimony collected thirty years later. That makes it potentially more valuable than most Roswell sources. But the diary has not been published in full, and its exact contents remain disputed among researchers.

The confession that opened only after death

Walter Haut was the press officer who wrote the original 1947 release announcing the "flying disc." For the rest of his public life, he played down any extraterrestrial reading.

But he left a sworn affidavit to be opened only after his death. In 2007, following his passing, his family made it public. In it, Haut described seeing in Hangar 84 an oval metallic object three to four metres long, with no windows, no wings and no landing gear — and two small bodies roughly four feet tall.

He wrote that he was "convinced that what I personally observed was some type of craft and its crew from outer space."

If the testimony is truthful, there is no way to reconcile it with a weather balloon. A Mogul component is not a four-metre oval. It does not have occupants.

Why this case resists a conclusion

There are concrete reasons this case resists closure, beyond the sensationalism.

The General Accounting Office, which investigated formally in 1994, found incomplete records and broken chains of custody in the military documentation of the period. Many 1947 files were destroyed — either deliberately or as part of routine administrative procedure at the time. Seven decades on, there is no way to tell those two scenarios apart.

On top of that, most of the testimony about "alien bodies" was collected thirty years after the events, when human memory is already malleable and open to contamination from later media accounts. And not every witness has held up: Frank Kaufmann, a primary source for much of the classic Roswell narrative, was exposed in 2002 for forging documents and inventing stories over decades. His contributions have been discarded by serious researchers.

That does not invalidate the whole case. But it demands careful separation of which accounts survive scrutiny and which do not.

The hypothesis rarely discussed

There is a third explanation, cited far less often than "weather balloon" or "extraterrestrial craft": that the government did conceal something in 1947, but something of terrestrial origin. An experimental American aeronautical prototype, possibly derived from German technology captured after the Second World War. Or even a Soviet device that had penetrated airspace over a base with active nuclear capability.

At the height of the Cold War, admitting that foreign forces could overfly a US nuclear installation with impunity would have been politically devastating. This hypothesis explains the cover-up, the shifting official versions and the persistent classification of certain documents.

What it does not fully explain are the physical properties Marcel described. A conventional prototype, however secret, would still be made of known materials: metal that bends, that cuts, that burns.

What 2026 might — or might not — resolve

The Trump administration has promised further UAP declassification through 2026, and the PURSUE program has already released hundreds of files this year. The hope among researchers is that original 1947 documents, hidden until now, will finally surface.

There are structural reasons to temper that hope. Governments withhold classified information when its release could compromise intelligence methods still in use, reveal the identities of sources, expose institutional error, or contradict decades of established official narrative. None of those incentives has disappeared seventy-eight years later.

It is entirely possible that more documents are released in 2026 and that the central pieces of the puzzle — what exactly the material was, why Marcel described it as indestructible, what Haut actually saw in Hangar 84 — remain classified under the same argument as always: national security.

What Roswell taught, beyond what happened

Whatever it was, Roswell demonstrated something that outlived the incident itself: that a military institution can publicly announce the capture of a "flying saucer" and retract it the next day without offering an explanation that survives scrutiny — and that once that disconnect is sown, it is not forgotten.

Every time a government announces a new declassification on anomalous phenomena today, someone remembers Roswell and wonders whether this time will be different, or whether we will get the same sequence again: revelation, retraction, decades of silence.

The material from the Brazel ranch, whatever it was, still has no explanation that closes every question. Marcel's diary still has not been published in full. The 2025 video, by contrast, is solved — and it solved nothing about 1947. It was a photograph, filmed.

Seventy-eight years is a long time for a "closed" case to keep producing more questions than answers.

In November 2024, more than a thousand unidentified objects were reported over high-security military installations in the United States and Europe in under a month. The most advanced air defence systems in the world could not intercept them. No satisfactory official explanation has been given.

The program the Pentagon denies

In his congressional testimony, investigative journalist Michael Shellenberger presented evidence of an Unacknowledged Special Access Program — a USAP — called "Immaculate Constellation." According to his sources, the program consolidates UAP observations from multiple intelligence collection platforms — satellites, military sensors, drones — into a centralised system controlled directly by the White House and executed by the Department of Defense.

The disturbing part is not that it exists. It is the apparent purpose: evading legal congressional oversight. The Pentagon officially denies the program. Multiple internal sources have told Shellenberger it is real.

Senator Marco Rubio, then vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, has confirmed that individuals with first-hand knowledge of UAP programs have testified before Congress in classified sessions. That is the backdrop. What happened a few weeks later is the part that has never been explained.

The 2024 incursions: when the defences failed

Between November and December 2024, something without precedent happened over American and European military installations. In New Jersey alone, more than a thousand sightings were reported in under a month. The objects were described as roughly the size of an SUV, with bright pulsing lights, and they behaved in ways that did not match any known platform:

  • They held position in extreme wind
  • They changed colour: white, orange, blue, green
  • They operated with no detectable thermal signature
  • They evaded radar, infrared systems and electronic countermeasures
  • They reached speeds above 100 km/h
  • They deliberately went dark when surveillance systems approached

The most troubling detail: an officer at Langley Air Force Base revealed that the military had been warned about the incursions a year in advance. They had twelve months to prepare. The objects evaded every defence anyway.

The objects carried no lights as they approached the bases. They switched them on deliberately on arrival, as if announcing themselves: "I am here."

The speed nobody wants to talk about

One number in that list deserves more attention than it gets, and it cuts against the spectacular reading rather than for it.

Speeds "above 100 km/h" are not extraordinary. They are the speed of a car on a motorway. Nothing in the reported performance envelope of the 2024 wave requires physics we do not have — no instantaneous acceleration, no transmedium travel, none of the signatures that make the Nimitz encounter hard to dismiss.

What is hard to explain is not how fast these objects moved. It is that slow, low-flying, visually obvious objects operated for weeks over the most heavily defended airspace on earth, after a year of warning, and nothing stopped them. The anomaly here is institutional, not aerodynamic — and that is a more uncomfortable finding, not a lesser one.

The phenomenon was global

What happened in New Jersey was not isolated. Similar incidents were documented simultaneously in Brazil, Sudan, Portugal, Japan, Iran and Thailand. In Japan, an airport was closed. In Iran, airspace restrictions were imposed.

That phenomena with identical characteristics appeared across multiple continents, in populations with little shared access to American social media, argues against mass hysteria or media contagion. This was not a viral panic. It was something that happened in several places at once.

It is worth being precise about what that does and does not establish. Simultaneity across borders rules out a single national panic. It does not, by itself, identify what was in the sky — a global phenomenon can still be a global technology.

The dilemma with no comfortable answer

The US government faces three possible explanations, each more awkward than the last.

If these objects are secret American technology, why would it allow them to disrupt military operations, close civilian runways and demonstrate total control of national airspace for weeks?

If they are foreign adversaries — China or Russia — why have no decisive defensive measures been taken after months of documented incursions over critical nuclear and military installations?

If they are non-human, what arrangements or understandings exist that would explain the absence of a military response to repeated violations of the most protected airspace in the world?

President Donald Trump has publicly claimed the government "knows what they are" and "where they come from," without providing detail. The mass declassification his administration ordered in May 2026 has opened more questions than it has closed.

What is known and what nobody explains

Immaculate Constellation may or may not exist. The 2024 incursions did happen — they are documented by the militaries of several countries. The defences failed — that is documented too.

What remains without official explanation is not whether these events occurred, but what caused them, and why twelve months of warning were not enough to stop them.

It is also not the first time. Objects over nuclear and strategic installations, no interception, no identification, and a documentary record that confirms the pattern while explaining none of it, is a sequence that runs back six decades. What changed in 2024 is not the pattern. It is that it happened in front of a thousand phone cameras.

There is a question governments have sidestepped for decades: if UAP are simply drones, balloons or atmospheric effects, why do they keep turning up over the most dangerous places on the planet?

PURSUE: the largest UAP release on record

On 8 May 2026 the Trump administration opened a door that had been shut for decades. PURSUE — the Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters — published a first tranche at WAR.GOV/UFO: 158 documents across 162 files, mixing PDFs, video and imagery. On 22 May a second tranche added 64 more, bringing the public total to 222 documents. No password, no clearance.

The coordination involved at least seven federal bodies: the White House, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, the Department of Energy, AARO, NASA, the FBI and additional intelligence community components. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth framed the goal as "maximum transparency."

The Pentagon put traffic to the portal at more than a billion visits in its first two weeks. The world had been waiting for this.

What nobody expected was how much of it concerned nuclear installations.

The morning a flight went dark

On 16 March 1967, at 8:30 in the morning, something happened in Montana that Strategic Air Command could not explain.

All ten Minuteman missiles of Echo Flight at Malmstrom Air Force Base lost strategic alert within ten seconds of one another. In the classified message that circulated afterwards, SAC wrote that "the fact that no apparent reason for the loss of ten missiles can be readily identified is cause for grave concern."

Eight days later it happened again, at a different flight and at night. Deputy Missile Combat Crew Commander Robert Salas took a call at Oscar Flight from his security controller: a pulsating red-orange object, roughly forty feet across, was hovering directly outside the front gate of the launch control facility. Minutes later, six to eight of his ten missiles dropped to "No-Go." Inoperable. No physical damage. No technical explanation.

The arithmetic is worth stating plainly, because it circulates wrong: ten at Echo Flight, six to eight of ten at Oscar Flight. The documented total is between sixteen and eighteen missiles, not the twenty often quoted. The full case is in our analysis of Malmstrom 1967.

Boeing investigated for months. Its conclusion was as unsettling as the incident: only an externally introduced voltage pulse could reproduce the effect, and it was "impossible for any normal device to affect all ten missiles simultaneously."

And Malmstrom was not an isolated case.

At Minot Air Force Base in North Dakota, Lieutenant David Schuur described a large metallic object moving slowly over the launch facilities at low altitude. What caught his attention was not simply its presence: as the object passed over each silo, the systems reported "spurious indications" on that missile specifically — and only that one. As if it were reading them one by one.

At the worst moment, "launch in progress" indicators appeared. Operators had to throw inhibit switches manually to prevent an unauthorised launch of nuclear missiles.

In October 1968, a B-52 crew described an object roughly 200 feet across with rotating lights that paced the bomber for 20 nautical miles before departing vertically. All personnel involved were placed under non-disclosure orders.

Vandenberg: a beam against a nuclear warhead

In September 1964, Air Force officer Bob Jacobs was operating a high-resolution photographic system at Vandenberg Air Force Base in California, filming a test launch of an Atlas D missile.

What he recorded was not in the script.

On the 16mm film, a disc-shaped object approached the test warhead — travelling at over 14,000 km/h at the edge of space — and struck it four times with some kind of beam. The warhead veered off into the ocean, falling hundreds of kilometres short of its intended target.

Jacobs described what happened as "a shot across the bow": a demonstration of capability. Not a destruction. A warning.

In May 2014, multiple bright objects were reported manoeuvring near the Vandenberg perimeter during preparations for a Minuteman III launch test. The test was scrubbed with no official explanation.

Loring: three hundred metres from the weapons

In October 1975, something entered restricted airspace at Loring Air Force Base in Maine and closed to within 300 metres of bunkers holding active nuclear weapons.

The object was described as metallic, elongated, with no visible means of propulsion. The base went to maximum security. The CIA, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the National Security Agency were notified immediately. The craft evaded every interception attempt and left at unprecedented speed.

Over the following four days, similar objects kept appearing. Strategic Air Command and ICBM bases across the country went to heightened alert.

Nobody intercepted anything. Nobody identified anything.

The Soviet Defence Ministry file was sealed.

Half a century. Two superpowers. Multiple bases. Missiles knocked off alert, launch sequences triggered, a warhead pushed off course. No satisfactory technical explanation in any of them.

Three hypotheses remain open: adversary technology so advanced that no government will admit to it; unknown physical phenomena that interact selectively with electronic systems; or something that has spent decades systematically monitoring the most dangerous things we have built.

The files do not say which is right. They do confirm the question is a legitimate one.

When Verónica reached Netflix in 2017 it arrived with a line of marketing that did more work than the film did: it was based on the only case in Spanish history in which a police report officially documented paranormal activity. The line travelled further than the picture. It is still the first thing written about the Vallecas case in English, and it is repeated as the hard residue of the story — the part that survives once the ouija board and the teenage ghost are stripped away.

It is worth noticing, before anything else, that the film gets the order of events backwards. Verónica shows a girl haunted and then killed: the phenomena come first and the death is their conclusion. The documents describe the opposite sequence. Estefanía Gutiérrez Lázaro died in the summer of 1991. The events her family reported — and the night four officers came to the flat — begin more than a year afterwards. That inversion is not a small liberty. It is the whole causal claim of the case, and the film supplies it in a direction the paperwork does not support.

So we went looking for the paperwork. The word "paranormal" is not in it.

A word that does not exist in English

Start with the translation, because this is where the English version of the claim gets its strength.

The document is not a "police report". It is an atestado, and Spanish criminal procedure defines it precisely. Article 297 of the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal states that an atestado drawn up by the judicial police has the legal status of a complaint — a denuncia. It is not proof. It is the instrument that opens an investigation, not one that concludes anything.

Its internal structure makes the point even more clearly. An atestado is a chain of numbered diligencias, and one of them is the diligencia de inspección ocular: the section where an officer sets down what he personally observed at the scene. It is, by design, the place in the document where a policeman writes "I saw this" without that meaning "this is what happened".

English has no single word for that. "Police report" implies a finding. "Official document" implies a determination. Neither carries the legal meaning that every Spanish lawyer takes for granted, which is that an atestado records what was reported and observed and settles nothing. So the claim arrives in English already inflated, and the inflation is invisible unless you know the statute.

What the document actually says

A caveat first, because it is the whole point of this piece: the atestado has never been published. There is no scan, no PDF, no case number in circulation. What circulates is paraphrase, and paraphrase of paraphrase.

The passages below come from an article by Jesús Logueira Álvarez, published in June 2022 on Una historia de la Policía Nacional, a Spanish National Police history blog, which states that it took the text from "Emblema", the force's own bulletin. That is a transcription rather than the original — but it is a chain of custody that can be named, which is more than any of the newspaper accounts offer. The same article carries scene photographs attributed to the force's forensic unit.

With that stated, here is the text, in our translation:

"The situation was one of mystery and strangeness; that, sitting in the company of the whole family, they were able to hear and observe how the door of a wardrobe, perfectly shut — which they verified afterwards — opened suddenly and in a wholly unnatural manner, which prompted serious suspicions in the Chief Inspector and the three officers present."
"That they had not recovered from the surprise and, while discussing it, a loud noise occurred on the terrace, where they were able to confirm that nobody was there, so that the aforementioned suspicions increased and were reinforced, the event taking on an unsuspected interest."
"That moments later they were able to notice and observe how, on the small table holding the telephone and specifically on a doily, a brown stain of some consistency appeared, which the Chief Inspector identifies as drool."

That chained "That…" construction is the standard form of Spanish diligencias, and on its own it is the best evidence that this is the text rather than somebody's summary of it.

Now read what is there. Four officers — a chief inspector and three others. A closed wardrobe door that opens "in a wholly unnatural manner". A noise with nobody on the terrace. A stain the chief inspector calls drool.

And read what is not there. Not paranormal. Not supernatural, poltergeist, ghost, spirit or haunting. The document's vocabulary is mystery, strangeness, unnatural, suspicions — the language of a man describing what he saw and flagging that it did not add up. It is not the language of a man assigning a cause.

"The only one in Spain" cannot be checked by anyone

There is a second half to the famous sentence that deserves separating out, because it is repeated even more often than the first: that this is the only atestado of its kind in the whole of Spain.

How would anybody establish that? No index of atestados by content exists. There is no database in which you can request "reports mentioning inexplicable events" and find that exactly one comes back. Atestados are filed by proceeding, by court and by date — not by how strange their contents are. To sustain "it is the only one" you would have to have read all the others.

We are not saying the claim is false. We are saying that, as phrased, it cannot be tested by anyone who wanted to test it. A claim that cannot be put to the question is not a strong claim; it is an unexamined one. It has been circulating for three decades with the appearance of a fact.

What the atestado can and cannot settle

It can establish that in the early hours of 27 November 1992 four officers attended a flat on Calle Luis Marín in Puente de Vallecas, and that one of them wrote down that they had witnessed things he could not explain. That is signed and filed, and it is more than exists in almost any comparable case anywhere.

It cannot establish what caused them. An atestado is not an expert report; the inspección ocular records observation, not diagnosis. The chief inspector has since described himself as a sceptic and spoken of there being "twenty thousand explanations" — remarks that circulate in fragments and deserve to be read whole before anyone leans on them, which is precisely the error this article is about.

And it says nothing whatsoever about the death of Estefanía Gutiérrez Lázaro fifteen months earlier. It is a document about one night in a flat in November 1992. The chain that the films and the podcasts rely on — ouija board, possession, death — is not in the paper. It was assembled around it.

Where this document had to go

Here is a question nobody has asked in thirty-four years, and it has an answer.

An atestado does not stay in the police station. The law requires it to move. Article 284 directs the judicial police, on learning of an offence, to report it to the judicial authority or to the public prosecutor. Article 295 puts a clock on it:

"Under no circumstances may officers of the Judicial Police allow more than twenty-four hours to pass without giving notice to the judicial authority or the Public Prosecutor of the proceedings they have carried out."

(We quote the consolidated text in force; the exact 1992 wording would need checking, which is precisely the kind of verification this article is asking for.)

What that means is concrete. If the atestado exists — and four officers signed it — then within twenty-four hours somebody had to notify a court or a prosecutor. There is, or was, a registry number, a date of entry, and an investigating magistrate in Madrid with this on the desk.

The question therefore stops being atmospheric. It is no longer "whatever became of that report?" but: which court received it, under what number, and what was decided? It is widely repeated that the case was shelved "for lack of coherence", and not one source names the court, the date or the ruling. That sentence travels on its own, with nothing behind it.

This matters more than it looks. If the file followed its legal course, there is a documentary trail independent of the police that would confirm or demolish half of what is told about this case in an afternoon. Nobody has gone to look. Thirty-four years of programmes, books, a feature film and two documentaries, and the most obvious check remains undone.

What is still open

The document. No case number, no station, no signature has ever been published. What exists is a transcription that is convincing in its form, not the original.

The painting. The same police source reproduces a photograph of a picture that "began to burn spontaneously, with no traces of accelerants found". This deserves a pause: it is the only element of the case that resembles evidence in the ordinary sense. Testimony tells you what somebody saw; an analysis tells you what a sample contains. If the forensic unit looked for accelerants and did not find them, that is a negative measurement — the kind of result that does not depend on anyone's memory.

On March 5, 2004, a Mexican Air Force aircraft on a routine patrol against drug smuggling recorded a string of brilliant lights on its infrared camera that the crew could not see out of the window. At one point there were eleven. Two months later the footage, handed over by Mexico's own Secretary of Defense to a television journalist, went public, and "eleven UFOs over Campeche" became a worldwide story.

It is an unusual case: a military released sensor footage of unidentified objects on its own initiative. It is also one of the better-solved ones, because the video itself carries the data needed to work out where the camera was pointing. What makes Campeche worth revisiting is not only what the lights were, but how that was established, who got it wrong along the way, and which questions are still open more than twenty years later.

A drug patrol and a camera that sees heat

The aircraft was a Merlin C-26A belonging to Mexico's Secretariat of National Defense (SEDENA). According to the analysis Robert Sheaffer published in the September/October 2004 issue of Skeptical Inquirer, it was flying at about 3,500 meters (roughly 11,500 feet) over the states of Chiapas and Campeche when its infrared system began recording very bright points of light.

That system was a Star SAFIRE II, built by FLIR Systems of Portland, Oregon. It does not record visible light but heat: it detects radiation in the 3-to-5-micron band and builds its picture from differences in temperature and emissivity across the scene. On a display like that, anything hotter than its background looks as if it were glowing on its own.

That technical detail is the key to the whole case. The Campeche lights could not be seen with the naked eye; they existed only in the infrared. For those who argued the objects were anomalous, that was proof of something extraordinary. For infrared specialist John Lester Miller, whom Sheaffer consulted, it meant the opposite. "These systems are specifically designed to detect objects that cannot be seen by the human eye," he explained. "If the eye could see everything that the IR sensor can, then it would be far cheaper and more effective to put a few privates in the aircraft with binoculars."

The footage was not leaked. It was handed over by General Clemente Ricardo Vega García, head of SEDENA, to Jaime Maussan, a broadcaster with the Mexican network Televisa. The choice of recipient shaped everything that followed. Sheaffer, an avowed skeptic, describes Maussan as a broadcaster known for sensational promotion of supposed mysteries, and notes that columnist Raúl Trejo Delarbre made the same point in La Crónica de Hoy on May 17: anyone wanting an objective evaluation of the video would not have started there.

The Bay of Campeche is the heart of Mexico's oil industry. It is home to Cantarell, the country's largest oil field, about 80 kilometers offshore. The argument most often made for the case was that the aircraft's radar had also picked up objects. Campeche is often compared with the USS Nimitz encounter, also from 2004 and also known for a military infrared video. The comparison is useful mainly for what it shows Campeche lacks. What the two share is a lesson about sensors: an infrared video on its own gives neither range nor size. As Miller put it, the range of an object cannot be inferred from its brightness or size in the image unless the atmosphere and the sensor settings are known. That is why such systems often carry a laser rangefinder, and the one on the Merlin did not. A bright point can be something small and close or something enormous and far away, and the image cannot tell you which.

The oil-flare explanation is strong. It rests on the data in the video itself, it was reached independently by a UFO researcher who knew the area, and it matches what appears when the zoom is closed. But the case leaves open questions, none of which involve aliens:

  • The data that was never released. SEDENA refused the UNAM researchers the technical flight data on the grounds that it was classified, while the video was being shown on television. Every later analysis has been built on what the screen shows, not on the aircraft's records.
  • The radar returns. The trucks explain some of them, by Sparks's own account, not all. And with no onboard radar recording, that can no longer be checked.
  • Why it was released that way. A defense secretary chose to make military footage public by giving it to a promoter of the paranormal rather than to a technical team. The general's explanation, that he saw no point in keeping it stored away, leaves open why he chose that recipient.

Campeche 2004 is remembered as the case of the Mexican Air Force's eleven UFOs. Read through the documents, it is something more useful: a near-textbook example of how a military sensor can turn something ordinary into a mystery, how hasty explanations from both sides muddied the debate, and how the answer was in the numbers in the corner of the screen all along.

The other two are why this document is worth reading. The third is to use plants, "including transgenic plants", as biosensors for radiation and magnetic-field deviations. The experiments, which are real experiments

On 6 April 2010 the team brought six spiderwort and six tobacco plants to the ranch, three of each in two different rooms, under grow lights cycling sixteen hours on and eight off. The tobacco assay is not improvised. It uses a naturally occurring mutation at the Sulfur gene: heterozygous Su/+ plants are pale green, and exposure to a mutagenic agent shows up as visible spots — dark green where one allele is knocked out, white or yellow where the other is. This is a mutagenesis bioassay used in actual laboratories.

On 22 April they set up a germination experiment in three places at once: two rooms of the ranch trailer and a control at BAASS's own facility. Sugar snap peas and green beans, forty-nine seeds of each type per tray, in reusable elongated pots under timed grow-light systems, watered with distilled water at every location. That is a competent design: it has a control, it fixes its environmental variables, and it asks a falsifiable question. What it does not have yet is a result, and the report says so plainly — at the time of the April deployment the plants had been at the ranch for only two weeks, so "no observations or analysis can be drawn" about mutation levels in either species.

The instrumental finding that will travel furthest from this document is also the one it explains most carefully. Put plainly: a contractor measured unexplained signals sitting in the bands where satellite navigation lives, wrote that down, and recorded that it had not ruled out the obvious. Nowhere does the document assert that the signals were anomalous.

Three things, and the report leaves them open itself. The DNA analysis of the hair samples was in progress and no result appears here. The track photographs remain unidentified. And both botanical experiments had barely started.

What is now on the record, with dates, equipment models and a catalogue number, is what a slice of the DIA's AAWSAP money actually bought: ten days in the field across two deployments, seven trail cameras, more than twenty-five interviews, twelve biosensor plants, three trays of ninety-eight seeds each, and a spectrum sweep. For a programme whose shape has been reconstructed largely from testimony, that is a different kind of source.

It should not be stretched past what it holds. This report does not establish that anything its witnesses describe exists, and it does not try to; it is a status update on an ongoing investigation, written for the client paying for it. Its documentary value is in the method and in what the method reveals about the programme's priorities — not in its conclusions, because it reaches none.

Between February 2001 and March 2002, a Scottish systems administrator in a north London flat used a dial-up modem and a short Perl script to get inside 97 United States military and NASA computers. That part is not disputed. He admitted it at the police station, and the United Kingdom and the United States spent the next ten years arguing about what to do with him.

What he says he found inside is a different kind of claim, and the distinction between those two things is the whole story.

The Method Was Embarrassingly Simple

Gary McKinnon, born in Glasgow in 1966, had become fixated on finding evidence of a government UFO cover-up. The trigger was testimony by Donna Hare, a former NASA contractor who claimed the agency routinely airbrushed anomalous objects out of its imagery before public release. If that were true, McKinnon reasoned, the unretouched originals had to sit on a server somewhere.

Operating under the handle "Solo", he ran a script that tried default credentials against networked machines belonging to the Department of Defense, the Navy, Air Force, Army, NASA and the Pentagon. By his own estimate he found roughly 5,000 computers with no password set at all.

That is the part of the case with the firmest ground under it, and it is genuinely damning — of the defender, not the intruder. The world's best-funded military had left thousands of networked machines open to anyone who tried the obvious.

On NASA's Johnson Space Center systems, McKinnon described four folders: Filtered, Unfiltered, Processed and Raw. In one labelled for fleet imagery, he opened a file. He has no copy of any of it. No screenshot, no download, no file. Everything above is his recollection, given afterwards, of material he was unable to take with him. The folder names are the part most often cited as self-evidently damning. They are also the part with an available ordinary explanation.

We now know, from the Pentagon's own catalogue published in the sixth PURSUE release, that the Defense Intelligence Agency did commission papers on antigravity, warp drive, traversable wormholes and metamaterials — 37 of them, between 2009 and 2011, under a programme called AAWSAP. The department describes them in its own metadata as "reference and synthesis products rather than original research": commissioned literature reviews of published physics.

Those particular papers postdate McKinnon's intrusion by eight years, so they are not what he saw. The point is narrower and it cuts both ways: a US government file directory containing the word "antigravity" is now a documented, unremarkable thing, and it does not by itself imply recovered hardware. "Non-Terrestrial Officers" is harder to account for benignly, and nobody has produced an innocent reading of it. It is also, like everything else, unverifiable.

Arrested in March 2002 — traced through the AOL email address he had used to download software — McKinnon faced six months to four years under UK law. In November 2002 the United States indicted him on seven counts under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, with a theoretical maximum of 70 years. A prosecutor said publicly that he wanted to see him "fry".

The claimed damage, between $700,000 and $900,000, consisted largely of the cost of auditing the compromised systems.

His supporters have long argued that the severity is itself evidence: that no one pursues a man this hard over an empty finding. It is an understandable inference and a weak one. Post-9/11 CFAA prosecutions were routinely severe, headline maximum sentences bear little relation to actual outcomes, and a military that had just been shown to have thousands of unprotected machines had its own institutional reasons to make an example. Embarrassment and concealment produce the same behaviour from the outside.

What followed were ten years of legal limbo under extreme bail conditions — nightly police check-ins, curfew, a total internet ban. He lost his job and his relationship, and by his own account stopped looking after himself.

In 2008 an autism specialist who saw him interviewed on television contacted his solicitor. The diagnosis reframed the obsessive search and the apparent inability to anticipate how his actions would be read.

The case also exposed the asymmetry of the 2003 bilateral extradition treaty. To extradite from the United States, Britain had to show probable cause; to extradite from Britain, the United States needed only reasonable suspicion. Under the treaty, 130 people went one way and 54 the other.

On 16 October 2012, Home Secretary Theresa May told Parliament that extradition would carry "such a high risk of him ending his life that a decision to extradite would be incompatible with his human rights" — the first time a Home Secretary had blocked an extradition under the treaty on those grounds. In December, Keir Starmer, then Director of Public Prosecutions, announced there would be no UK trial either: the prospects of conviction were low and obtaining the evidence from the United States presented insurmountable difficulties.

McKinnon walked free with no conviction and no trial. An active Interpol notice means he still cannot leave the country.

What the Case Actually Settles

Two things are documented and neither is in dispute: that US military and NASA systems were trivially insecure in 2001, and that the response to the man who demonstrated it was disproportionate to the technical harm he caused. Both are real findings about institutions, and both would stand if he had found nothing at all.

What is not documented is the content. There is no file, no copy, no corroborating witness, and nothing in the subsequent two decades of disclosure — including the Immaculate Constellation material and the PURSUE releases — that references a fleet roster or the imagery he described.

It is tempting to read official silence as confirmation. It is not: no agency confirms or denies the contents of a compromised system as a matter of routine, and an absence of denial is produced equally by a cover-up and by there being nothing to deny. The honest position is that the intrusion is a matter of record and what he saw inside it is a matter of testimony — and that the first does not lend weight to the second.

It was a rhetorical device about the radical inclusivity of the Gospel, framed by Peter's vision in Acts, and its real subject was the Church's openness to those regarded as outsiders — with direct application to migrants.

But the underlying question was legitimate, and the implied answer was unambiguous: if a rational extraterrestrial being asked for baptism, there is no doctrinal impediment.

The history matters here, and not as decoration. In 1633 the Church condemned Galileo. In 1992 John Paul II formally called that judgment an error born of tragic mutual incomprehension. The institution that condemned Galileo is the institution that funds front-line astrophysics. That trajectory took four centuries and was not graceful, but it happened, and it is the reason the current openness is more than a press line.

Set against the eight decades of US government research we have covered, and the arguments now reaching Congress, the contrast is instructive rather than devotional.

One institution has spent decades being asked what it knows and answering in redactions — the PURSUE releases are the current instalment. The other was asked a harder question, about meaning rather than evidence, and answered it in print, under named authorship, in its own newspaper, with the reasoning shown.

Neither tells us whether anything is out there. But only one of them has published what it would think if there were.

On 11 October 1973, two men went fishing on the Pascagoula River in Mississippi. Three hours later they were in the sheriff's office, shaking, trying to describe something neither had words for.

What makes Pascagoula unusual is not the account itself — abduction reports are not rare. It is a reel of tape the two men did not know was running.

An Ordinary Evening on the Gulf Coast

Charles Hickson was 42 and worked at a service station. Calvin Parker was 19. That October evening they went to the pier beside the Ingalls shipyards, on the west bank of a river locals called the Singing River, after a low unexplained hum said to come off the water in certain conditions.

They were not the only ones who saw something that night. Hours earlier a gas station worker, Larry Booth, had reported a craft with a flashing light to the sheriff. October 1973 fell inside an unusually heavy sighting wave across the American South — Louisiana, Mississippi, Tennessee — that had been generating reports for weeks.

At around 8 p.m., a buzzing or hissing noise broke the quiet. Two flashing blue lights approached from the sky. The source, they said, was an oval object roughly 9 to 12 metres across and 2 to 3 metres high, descending silently over the water.

Three figures emerged. They floated rather than walked. Hickson described them as about 1.8 metres tall, with greyish wrinkled skin he compared to an elephant's, bullet-shaped heads, slit mouths, and limbs ending in something like pincers.

Both men said they were paralysed — conscious but unable to move — and were taken aboard. Parker lost consciousness almost immediately and stayed out for most of it. Hickson did not. He described a bare metal interior, cold, lit from no visible source, and a floating device he called "a giant mechanical eye" passing slowly over his body. About thirty minutes later, he said, they were returned to the pier.

The two men went first to the Mississippi Press, found it empty, and continued to the Jackson County Sheriff's Office, arriving near 11 p.m. Captain Glenn Ryder received them sceptically — he smelled whiskey on Hickson, who said he had drunk after the encounter to steady himself. Officers judged neither man intoxicated at the time of the report.

Then the investigators did something that decided the case's standing for the next fifty years: they left Hickson and Parker alone in an interview room with a hidden recorder running. The expectation was ordinary police work — that two men who had just fabricated a story would relax, slip, contradict each other.

The tape, rediscovered decades later, shows the opposite. Two men alone, believing no one was listening, remain frightened. They worry aloud about their families. Their accounts stay consistent with each other and with what they had told the officers. There is no moment of release, no suggestion of performance.

It is worth being precise about what that establishes, because this is where most accounts overreach. The tape is strong evidence that Hickson and Parker believed what they were saying. It is not evidence of what happened on the pier. Sincerity and accuracy are different claims, and a recording can only reach the first.

The Mississippi Press ran the first report on 12 October; AP and UPI carried it nationally the same day. Both men were examined at Keesler Air Force Base for radiation exposure, with negative results.

The aftermath was hard on both men. Media pressure was immediate — Hickson appeared on the Dick Cavett, Mike Douglas and Tonight shows. In January 1974 he was arrested for public drunkenness on a highway.

Those objections deserve weight, and the escalation is the strongest of them: a witness whose account grows over decades is a witness whose early account also becomes harder to isolate. What they do not address is the recording made that first night, before any of the escalation, before the television appearances, and before either man knew anyone was listening.

What there is, uniquely, is a contemporaneous recording of the witnesses off-guard, which is the one thing a fabricated account tends not to survive.

Why No Official File Exists — And Never Will

Spain is the country that actually completed a UFO disclosure. Between 1992 and 1999 the Spanish Air Force released 84 files covering 122 sightings, close to 2,000 pages, scanned and published without an access procedure. Those files contain signed statements from military pilots and air traffic controllers, radio transcripts, hand-drawn witness sketches and meteorological reports.

The sightings in that archive run from 1962 to 1995. Los Villares happened in July 1996 — after the last incident the programme covered, and while the release itself was already in its final years. There was never going to be a Spanish Air Force bundle on Dionisio Ávila, because by then the Air Force had stopped building them for public release.

This matters more than it sounds. Compare it to the Manises incident of 1979, where a commercial flight diverted to Valencia: that case produced a 142-page Ministry of Defence report, declassified in 1994, with radar data and crew debriefings that independent researchers can still read and argue about. Los Villares produced testimony, symptoms, and eight rocks. Everything we know about it comes from the witness and from the researchers who interviewed him.

What the Witness Reported

Ávila described a semi-spherical, intensely luminous object moving toward the Jabalcuz area. His dog, which walked with him routinely, panicked immediately.

The object, as he described it, was four to five metres across, ovoid, with a metallic surface that reflected light and a crown of red and white lights on its upper section. Engraved on the surface was a mark he rendered as "IOI" — a stroke, a zero, a stroke.

Then three figures appeared. Bald, with no visible body hair and no ears, wearing a black suit fitted like a second skin. Two struck him as female, one as male. One of the female figures looked at him with what he read as anger; another made a gesture he took for a greeting.

When he looked up, the figures and the object were gone. Around his dog's neck was a cord holding a blue stone, which had not been there when they left the house.

The Part That Is Harder to Wave Away

Ávila developed severe weakness in his left shoulder. He saw specialists; clinical tests came back normal, everything "correct and healthy" by conventional measures. The loss of strength persisted anyway, with unusual pain and recurrent fevers that no doctor could attribute to a cause.

His car began to fail with no evident mechanical explanation. Researchers who documented the case reported a pattern: the failures happened specifically when he drove through the area where the object had vanished — the unavoidable route to his workplace — and not on other journeys.

The eight stones are the case's most tangible element and its most contested one. Unlike the overwhelming majority of sightings, which leave nothing but an account, here there are physical objects that could in principle be examined.
