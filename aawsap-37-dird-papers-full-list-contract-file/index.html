The 37 AAWSAP Papers: What the Department's Own Catalogue Says

The sixth PURSUE tranche was reported as the Pentagon's warp-drive papers. It is a contract file. We counted the catalogue: the department says 38 DIRDs and published 37, the seven procurement documents nobody listed name BAASS as the contractor, and all 37 papers are filed to Las Vegas.

The sixth PURSUE tranche was reported almost everywhere as the release of the Pentagon's papers on warp drives, wormholes and antigravity. That is a fair description of the subject matter and a poor description of the documents. What the Department of War actually published on 18 September 2026 is a contract: the statement of objectives, the award instrument, five modifications, and the deliverables that contract bought.

We went through the department's own catalogue — the uap-data.csv file that builds its portal — and counted. Three things came out of it that have not been reported, and the first one is the department contradicting itself.

The Catalogue Says 38. The Release Contains 37.

Every one of the Defense Intelligence Reference Documents in this tranche carries the same descriptive note, and that note says each is "one of 38 DIRDs produced under the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP) between 2009 and 2011."

We counted 37.

This is not a numbering gap. The identifiers run DOW-UAP-D117 through DOW-UAP-D153 — thirty-seven consecutive numbers with nothing missing between them. All 37 descriptive notes say 38, so it is not a typo in one record. The department counts a thirty-eighth document in the series and did not publish it in this tranche.

We are not going to tell you which one is missing, because we do not know and nothing in the public catalogue says. What is verifiable is the discrepancy itself, and that it comes from the department's own metadata rather than from anyone's inference.

What the 37 Actually Are

The catalogue is unusually direct about this. A DIRD, it says, is "a technical reference format used by the Defense Intelligence Agency to capture baseline knowledge on a specific topic for later analytic use," and the note adds the part that matters: DIRDs "are best understood as reference and synthesis products rather than as original research."

That is the department describing its own documents as literature reviews. It also concedes that "not every DIRD in the series directly concerns aerospace systems or future threat assessment" — which is visible from the titles alone, where biomaterials, biosensors and the Drake equation sit alongside propulsion.

The Part Nobody Counted: the Contract Itself

Seven documents in this tranche are not research at all. They are the procurement file, and as far as we can establish they have not been listed anywhere:

  • DOW-UAP-D110 — AAWSAP Statement of Objectives, 18 July 2008
  • DOW-UAP-D111 — Solicitation and Original Order, 22 September 2008
  • DOW-UAP-D112 — Contract Modification P00001, 2 September 2009
  • DOW-UAP-D113 — Contract Modification P00002, 2 February 2010
  • DOW-UAP-D114 — Contract Modification P00003, 18 May 2010
  • DOW-UAP-D115 — Contract Modification P00004, 18 May 2010
  • DOW-UAP-D116 — Contract Modification P00005, 28 September 2010

The catalogue's note on D111 names the contractor without hedging: it is "the original contract award instrument for AAWSAP work performed by Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, Inc. (BAASS), incorporating the contractor's proposal by reference and translating the earlier Statement of Objectives into priced contract line items."

It goes further, and this is the most useful sentence in the whole tranche for anyone trying to understand what AAWSAP was: the contract broke the base year into "monthly status reports, program management plans for the 12 technical areas, research reports, a comprehensive integrated threat assessment, travel, and other direct costs, and corresponding option-year line items."

Monthly status reports. Twelve management plans. An integrated threat assessment. That is the shape of a research services contract, and the 37 DIRDs are the research reports line item being delivered. The same programme produced the claims that reached Congress through later whistleblower testimony, which is a separate question from what the contract bought.

All Thirty-Seven Are Filed to Las Vegas

Every DIRD in the catalogue carries the same entry in its location field: Las Vegas, Nevada. All 37, without exception.

Read with the D111 note, that is the department placing the work at the contractor rather than at the agency — BAASS was headquartered in Las Vegas. It is a small metadata detail and it does more work than it looks: the documents were produced where the contract was performed, which is consistent with them being contract deliverables and not in-house intelligence product.

What This Does and Does Not Establish

It does not establish that anyone recovered anything. A paper titled Traversable Wormholes, Stargates, and Negative Energy is a commissioned review of published physics, written by an outside contractor, and the department says so in its own catalogue. The existence of a government document about warp drive is a fact about procurement, not about propulsion.

What it does establish is narrower and verifiable: a DIA-administered programme, active 2008 to 2012, with a 12-area scope and a more-than-40-year threat horizon, contracted to a named company in Nevada, which delivered a numbered series of reference papers between December 2009 and January 2011. The paperwork for all of it is now public, except for one document the department's own catalogue says exists.

That 37 of these had already been released through FOIA years earlier is something we covered when the tranche came out. The procurement file is the part that is genuinely new, and it is the part nobody led with.
