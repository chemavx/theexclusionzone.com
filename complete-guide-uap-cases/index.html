The Complete Guide to UAP Cases: Every Investigation, Every Case

Everything we know about unidentified aerial phenomena — organized by category, sourced from declassified records, military testimony, and investigative research. This guide covers all 67 cases and investigations published on The Exclusion Zone.

Military Encounters

Documented UAP events involving active military personnel, official investigations, and declassified defense records.

  • Nuclear Bases and UAP — From Malmstrom 1967 to Rendlesham 1980, declassified records show the same pattern repeating for six decades: objects reported over nuclear weapons sites, sometimes while the weapons themselves went down.
  • The 2024 Base Incursions — In late 2024 more than a thousand unidentified objects were reported over US and European military bases, and air defences stopped none of them despite a year of warning. What is documented, what is speculation, and why the anomaly is institutional.
  • Tehran 1976 — Two Iranian F-4 Phantoms lost instruments, weapons and communications as they closed on a bright object over the capital, and got everything back the moment they broke away. The Defense Intelligence Agency filed it as a textbook case and no explanation has followed in fifty years.
  • USS Russell 2019 — Navy destroyers off the Channel Islands were shadowed for two weeks of nights by objects nobody could identify. The leaked night-vision footage turned them into "flying pyramids" — that shape came from the camera lens, but the operators were never found.
  • Battle of Los Angeles 1942 — Fifty-eight minutes of anti-aircraft fire and 1,430 shells at something that never came down. Three officials gave three contradictory accounts within days, and the photograph everybody remembers was retouched before it ran.
  • Talavera la Real 1976 — Two Spanish conscripts emptied half a magazine at a three-metre figure inside an air base near Badajoz. The guard log recorded the exact time; the spent casings were never found, and the file sits in the declassified Air Force archive.
  • Kecksburg 1965 — An acorn-shaped object crashed into the Pennsylvania woods. NASA found nothing — until a FOIA lawsuit forced it to admit it had analyzed fragments, then lost the records.
  • Malmstrom 1967 — Two flights of Minuteman nuclear missiles went offline eight days apart while UFOs were reported overhead: sixteen to eighteen warheads in all. Boeing investigated and found no technical explanation.
  • Shag Harbour 1967 — Something fell into the sea off Nova Scotia in front of multiple witnesses. The Canadian Navy sent divers, found nothing, and the government logged the whole thing as unidentified in its own records.
  • Cash-Landrum 1980 — Three Texans, a diamond-shaped craft and radiation-like injuries. The only UFO encounter ever litigated in a U.S. federal court — dismissed without ruling on what happened.
  • Rendlesham Forest 1980 — Three nights of encounters at a NATO air base in Suffolk, England. The most documented military UAP case in European history.
  • JAL 1628 (1986) — Fifty minutes over Alaska, tracked simultaneously by onboard radar, air traffic control and military radar. The best-documented encounter in aviation history.
  • Belgium 1989–1990 — Belgian F-16s got radar locks on objects making impossible maneuvers and never saw a thing. The raw data is still classified.
  • USS Nimitz 2004 — Navy pilots intercept a white tic-tac shaped object off the California coast. The video that forced the Pentagon to acknowledge UAP.
  • GIMBAL and GOFAST — Five years after the Pentagon released both videos they remain officially unidentified — and the radar data and full telemetry that would settle them has never been declassified.

Classic Cases

The cases that shaped public understanding of UAP encounters — investigated, contested, and never fully resolved.

  • Kenneth Arnold 1947 — Nine objects over Mount Rainier, a pilot's simile about how they moved, and a reporter who turned it into a shape. Two weeks before Roswell, one misquote gave the century the words "flying saucer".
  • Roswell 1947 — An Army press release that spoke of a flying disc, retracted the next day, and eight decades of official explanations that never quite fit.
  • Chiles-Whitted 1948 — Two Eastern Air Lines pilots watched a windowed, cigar-shaped craft pass their DC-3 over Alabama. The Air Force investigation that followed helped push its own analysts toward a conclusion it would spend decades walking back.
  • Levelland 1957 — In one night, fifteen unconnected drivers spread across twenty miles of Texas told police the same story: engines dead, headlights out, a glowing object by the road, and everything working again the moment it left. The Air Force closed it as ball lightning.
  • Betty & Barney Hill 1961 — The first widely documented alien abduction case in the United States. Recovered under hypnosis, disputed ever since.
  • Ariel School 1994 — Sixty-two children in Ruwa, Zimbabwe described a landed craft and its occupants. Thirty years on, scattered across the world, they have never recanted — and there is still no physical evidence.
  • Vilas-Boas 1957 — A Brazilian farmer reported the template abduction four years before Betty and Barney Hill. The debunking everyone cites rests on an anachronism.
  • Travis Walton 1975 — A forestry worker vanished in front of six colleagues in Arizona. The crew chief later confessed it was a hoax — then retracted.
  • Pascagoula 1973 — Investigators left two frightened fishermen alone in an interview room with a hidden recorder running, expecting them to slip. The tape shows the opposite — which is strong evidence that they believed what they were saying, and none at all about what happened on the pier.
  • Phoenix Lights 1997 — Over one million witnesses across Arizona and Nevada. The governor who mocked it publicly later admitted he saw it too.
  • Socorro 1964 — A police officer broke off a traffic pursuit and found an egg-shaped craft on legs with two small figures beside it. Four depressions in caliche-hard soil, vegetation still smoldering, and a Blue Book verdict of "Unknown" that has never been revised.
  • Trans-en-Provence 1981 — A retired mason watched a grey disc settle on his terraced garden in southern France and leave again. France's national space agency spent two years on the marks it left, documented soil compression, trace metals and biochemical damage to the plants, and published the findings without an explanation.
  • Westall 1966 — Several hundred schoolchildren and teachers watched a metallic disc descend over a Melbourne suburb in broad daylight. The files were sealed, the witnesses were discouraged from talking, and Australia has never explained it.

Government Disclosure & Official Investigations

What the U.S. government has officially acknowledged, investigated, and released — and what remains classified.

  • Project Blue Book — 12,618 sightings over 22 years, 701 of them still unexplained, and a program that was quietly told to stop finding answers.
  • Project Stargate — Twenty million dollars and two decades of CIA and military remote viewing. The declassified files show what it actually produced.
  • AARO: Eight Decades of U.S. UFO Research — The Pentagon's own UAP office reviewed 80 years of classified programs and found no alien technology. Here's what they did find.
  • Immaculate Constellation — A 2024 whistleblower document describes a secret Pentagon UAP program. Nineteen months later it has been neither confirmed nor denied.
  • David Grusch at the Capitol — Three years after his sworn testimony: the claims, the congressional obstruction, and the declassification that still hasn't arrived.
  • AARO vs Grusch — The Pentagon office found no evidence any sighting was extraterrestrial; a year earlier a credentialed officer had sworn the opposite. A documented absence of evidence and an uncorroborated testimony are not symmetrical claims.
  • The Wilson-Davis Memo — Leaked notes describe a Deputy Director of the DIA being refused access to a classified reverse-engineering program, and warned off when he pressed. Their authenticity is still contested, and that is exactly what makes them worth reading carefully.

Declassified File Releases

The Pentagon's rolling UAP declassifications — what each drop actually contains, file by file.

  • Pentagon's Second UAP Declassification — 64 new files, Apollo 12 astronaut testimony, and 209 UAP sightings near nuclear facilities.
  • 222 Declassified UAP Files 2026 — Two releases, 222 files from WWII to 2026. Apollo astronaut testimony, submarine transmedium footage, and Cold War green orbs near nuclear facilities.
  • PURSUE Release 3 — A 1992 CIA confession that spy planes caused over half the UFO reports of the 1960s, active analysis of Apollo lunar photographs, and 294 files spanning 82 years.
  • PURSUE Release 4 — A 2015 intrusion at a nuclear weapons plant in Texas, a 1949 Los Alamos conference on unexplained "green fireballs", and a fresh look at an Apollo 17 photograph.
  • PURSUE Release 5 — A SECRET memo from 1948 in which the Air Force's own Director of Intelligence warns that "silence on our part will not long be acceptable" — released alongside sixteen videos, five of them with the time, coordinates and altitude blacked out.
  • PURSUE Release 6 — The department’s own catalogue listed 72 items, one more than the press counted, and 37 of them are AAWSAP papers on warp drives and wormholes that had been public via FOIA for years. The second time this has happened. Also inside: the 1952 Tremonton film and the Colorado police files, which went from five to eight with no announcement: the tranche is now 75.
  • PURSUE Release 6: the fifteen videos — We downloaded all fifteen clips and measured them. Three of the four results are negative, and it is the negatives that give the fourth its weight: redaction covers a median 10.3% of the frame in nine of the ten military videos, over the positions where sensor telemetry is rendered — consistent with a template applied in bulk rather than case-by-case judgement. The one clip with no redaction at all is the 1952 Tremonton film.
  • Utah Ranch 2010 — Sixty pages on the contractor's own letterhead, from the seventh release: two deployments to a ranch in Fort Duchesne, seven trail cameras, more than twenty-five interviews, and transgenic plants used as biosensors. It measures unexplained signals sitting in the satellite navigation bands, writes down that it has not ruled out the obvious, and concludes nothing — which is what makes it worth reading.
  • Space Vehicle Fragments 1962-1964 — Seventy-three pages from the seventh PURSUE release on who collects, analyzes and speaks about rocket and satellite debris that falls to earth. A 1963 memo records that the Defense Department and the Air Force wanted NASA as "cover", and the public draft deletes the word "analyzing".
  • The June 2026 Triangle — Two federal agencies filed on the same night: AARO's memorandum and the FBI's FD-302. The line everyone quotes about the case warranting further investigation is in neither — it comes from the catalogue blurb. We measured the nine photographs nobody was counting, and the two files do not even agree on how long it lasted: about thirty-two seconds against three.
  • Catalina Island 1949-1955 — Four files the Department of War catalogues alike as Unresolved UAP Report, all over one island. Read one by one they split in two, and neither half is the interesting kind: the 1955 card concludes "probable meteor" and an officer signs it, the 1953 file records Griffith Park astronomers blaming a planet seen after rain, and the other two simply had nothing to evaluate — one of them three Boy Scouts on a hillside.
  • The 37 AAWSAP Papers — The department's own catalogue says 38 DIRDs and the release contains 37, with no gap in the numbering. The full series with each file's identifier, the seven procurement documents nobody had listed — they name BAASS as the contractor — and all 37 filed to Las Vegas, where the company was and the agency was not.
  • The PURSUE Files and Nuclear Sites — Malmstrom, Minot, Vandenberg, Loring and the Soviet side of the Cold War: the May 2026 PURSUE release put decades of incidents over nuclear installations on the record. The files confirm the pattern. They do not explain it.

Latin America & Spain

Cases from the Spanish-speaking world — often overlooked in English-language UAP research, rigorously documented in military and government records.

  • Spain's UFO Files — Between 1992 and 1999 the Spanish Air Force declassified its entire military UFO archive: 84 files, 122 sightings, close to 2,000 pages, now online without an access procedure. Opening them made Spanish research more skeptical, not less.
  • UMMO 1966 — For nearly thirty years, typewritten letters from a planet called Ummo reached a Madrid circle of enthusiasts — until the man who wrote every page confessed. Spain's largest UFO hoax, and the three tests it failed from the first letter.
  • Colares 1977 — On an island in the Amazon delta, more than a hundred people walked into clinics with burns they blamed on beams of light from the sky. The Brazilian Air Force sent a detachment to photograph it, and its own files close without an answer.
  • Canary Islands 1976 — Over one million witnesses. A Spanish Navy corvette. Declassified files 18 years later and a Cold War secret the U.S. couldn't acknowledge.
  • Manises 1979 — The only commercial flight ever diverted by a UFO. A Mirage F1 scrambled at Mach 1.4 couldn't catch it.
  • Varginha 1996 — Brazil's Roswell. A creature captured by military in a small city, witness testimonies suppressed for years, and a cover-up that still hasn't fully unraveled.
  • Los Villares 1996 — A retiree in the mountains of Jaén reported three humanoids, eight stones engraved with the same "IOI" mark and symptoms that outlasted every medical test. The best-known contact case in the province, and the one Spanish case with no official file behind it.
  • Vallecas 1992 — The case behind the film Verónica is sold as the only Spanish police report to document paranormal activity. The atestado has never been published; the transcription that circulates speaks of "mystery and strangeness", and the word paranormal is not in it.
  • Chile's CEFAA Files — A 2014 Navy helicopter encounter on thermal video, analyzed by a military commission that published the entire file instead of burying it.
  • Campeche 2004 — A Mexican Air Force infrared camera recorded eleven lights the crew could not see out of the window, and the defense secretary handed the tape to television. The bearing data the camera printed on its own screen points straight at the gas flares of the Cantarell oil platforms.

Viral Claims, Myths & Forensic Analysis

Extraordinary claims put through the evidence — frame by frame, source by source. Sometimes they hold up. Often they don't.

  • Tassili N'Ajjer — "Ancient Aliens" calls the Round Head paintings proof of contact. Archaeology points to shamanic trance, psychoactive plants, and some very creative 1950s copies.
  • Area 51 in 2013 — A FOIA release forced the CIA to name and map the site. It turned out to be the institutional history of the U-2 and OXCART spy planes, not a UFO disclosure — and it documents the agencies issuing false explanations for sightings they had caused themselves.
  • Roswell's Viral "Crater" Video — Twenty-one minutes from the National Archives through a full forensic pipeline. The "crater with an alien body" turns out to be a camera filming a photograph in a book.
  • The Yellow Sea "Star" — PURSUE's six-pointed UAP video: chain of custody verified, then taken apart with a negative control and an optical simulation of the pattern.
  • The "Missing Scientists" Theory — How a YouTube video became a question at a White House press briefing in fourteen weeks. A study of the amplification chain, not a verdict.
  • The MJ-12 Documents — Papers describing a secret committee convened to handle crashed UFOs, which most researchers who have examined them consider forgeries. That verdict leaves the harder question standing: who made them, and what for?
  • The Vatican and Extraterrestrials — The popular assumption is that the Church and the search for alien life are natural enemies. Rome has run an observatory since 1582, staffs it with ten Jesuit astrophysicists who publish in peer-reviewed journals, and printed its answer in its own newspaper: «The extraterrestrial is my brother».
  • Gary McKinnon — A Scottish admin on a dial-up line got inside 97 US military and NASA machines using default passwords, and found some 5,000 with no password set at all. What he says he saw in there, including a roster of «Non-Terrestrial Officers», he could not take with him: there is no copy of any of it.
  • Spielberg's "Disclosure Day" — Fifty years after Close Encounters, Hollywood's most famous UFO skeptic built a film on declassified files and on-the-record testimony — and says he now believes.
