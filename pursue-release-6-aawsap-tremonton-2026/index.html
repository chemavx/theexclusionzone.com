PURSUE Release 6: Seventy-Two Files, and Thirty-Seven Were Already Public

The press counted 71 items in the Pentagon's sixth PURSUE tranche. The department's own catalogue lists 72 — and 37 of them, AAWSAP papers on warp drives and wormholes, had been public for years via FOIA. What's new: a Blue Book film from Tremonton, and police video from Colorado.

ChemaVX

On 18 September 2026 the Department of War published the sixth tranche of declassified files under PURSUE. The official announcement is signed by Sean Parnell, Assistant to the Secretary of War for Public Affairs and Chief Pentagon Spokesman, and it is brief: it states that the release is happening and points to WAR.GOV/UFO, where records continue to appear on a rolling basis.

Seventy-two items. More than half of them had been public for years.

What is in it

The press counted 71 items. The department's own catalogue — the uap-data.csv file that feeds its portal — lists 72: 67 from the Pentagon and five from local law enforcement in Colorado, comprising 56 PDF documents, 15 video clips and one audio recording. Those three figures total exactly 72, which is the first check worth running on any of these tranches. The gap is a single file: LLE-UAP-D001, the transcript that accompanies the Colorado videos and that the press counts left out.

⚠️ Not all the coverage gives the same number. Several outlets reported "ten new files" the same day. We do not know where that figure comes from: it may refer to what is genuinely unpublished, or it may be a wire error repeated down the chain. We say so rather than pick the number that reads best.

Thirty-seven of the seventy-two were already public

The bulk of the tranche is 37 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents — the technical papers commissioned by AAWSAP, the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program that the Defense Intelligence Agency set up in 2008.

The titles explain why they took the headlines: warp drives, traversable wormholes, antigravity, negative-mass propulsion, invisibility cloaking, spintronics. Read quickly, they look like the secret catalogue of a reverse-engineering programme.

Read carefully they are something else: commissioned literature reviews, written by outside contractors, on speculative physics published in open journals. A paper surveying what has been written about wormholes is not evidence that anyone has one.

And more to the point: all 37 had been available for years. The DIA itself had released them through Freedom of Information Act requests, and they have been circulating since in the files of the researchers who asked for them.

What is genuinely new in these copies is small and specific, and worth naming precisely because it is the only thing that changed: the versions published now restore details the agency had previously withheld, among them the names of the offices that produced each document. For anyone reconstructing an organisational chart, that is worth having. For anyone expecting recovered technology, there is nothing.

The second time this has happened

This is worth recording, because it is no longer a coincidence: it is the second PURSUE tranche whose most-discussed contents had been in the public domain for years.

The first was the third tranche in June, presented as the CIA's confession about its spy planes. The confession was real, and it had been published thirteen years earlier: the Pedlow and Welzenbach study came out through FOIA in 2013. We went further into that specific document in our piece on what the CIA actually admitted about Area 51.

Now, the 37 AAWSAP papers. The mechanism is identical: material that was already available enters a new portal, with a new stamp and a new date, and the coverage reads it as revelation. Nobody has to lie for that to happen. It is enough for the novelty of the container to be mistaken for novelty of the contents.

The honest question is not whether the Pentagon is deceiving anyone. It is more uncomfortable than that: how much of this archive is declassification, and how much is relocation? Absent an index marking what appears for the first time, the only way to know is to check document by document — and so far that work has been done by a handful of specialist reporters and almost nobody else.

Tremonton, 1952: the part that is genuinely archival

The tranche includes a video and a case file on an incident near Tremonton, Utah, on 2 July 1952, from the holdings of Project Blue Book.

The witness was Delbert C. Newhouse, a U.S. Navy warrant officer and professional Navy photographer. Newhouse described between twelve and fourteen reflective objects and filmed them on colour motion-picture film with a handheld Bell & Howell Auto Master camera.

The detail about his trade matters more than it appears. Newhouse was not a motorist with a phone: he was a military photographer with thousands of hours behind a camera, and the film became one of the few moving-image records Project Blue Book ever analysed seriously.

A connection worth checking

Here is something we have not seen flagged in the coverage, and we are putting it forward as a supported hypothesis, not as a fact.

The fifth tranche, in August, included a document catalogued as DOW-UAP-D098: a 1953 memorandum on the U.S. Naval Photographic Interpretation Center's assessment of "a film said to show unidentified airborne objects".

The Tremonton film was analysed by precisely that centre, and its report is one of the known pieces of the case file. That tranche five carried the 1953 memorandum about the analysis and tranche six carries the video and case file of the 1952 event fits together too neatly to leave unsaid.

But we are not treating it as confirmed, and we will say why: the tranche-five memorandum does not name the case in the caption the department published, and we have not been able to compare the two files. If they are the same case file split across two releases, that says something about how the archive is being portioned out. If they are not, then there are two separate films assessed by the same centre — which is also a fact worth having.

Colorado, October 2023

The remaining four files come from local law enforcement in Colorado and include video recorded in October 2023.

Local police material entering a federal anomalous-phenomena archive is, on its own, the quietest change of criteria in the whole series. For decades the system discarded by default any report that did not come from a military pilot or a radar return. This is not an endorsement of anything — a dashboard video gains no credibility by appearing on a Department of War portal. What it indicates is what now counts as worth keeping.

Where the series stands

With this tranche, the six releases run as follows:

  • 8 May — 158 documents (162 files)
  • 22 May — 64 more, to 222
  • 12 June — 72 more, to 294
  • 10 July — 40 more, to 334
  • 7 August — 41 more, to 375
  • 18 September — 72 more, to 447

And here, for the first time in the series, the count can be closed. Until now the first five figures came from the department's record index and the sixth from the press, which do not necessarily count the same unit, so adding them would have been manufacturing precision. But the file that feeds the portal — uap-data.csv — lists all six tranches in one place and in the same unit: 447 rows, which is exactly 158 + 64 + 72 + 40 + 41 + 72. The total no longer has to be inferred from press releases.

When the department publishes an updated index, we will check it and say so here. Update, 8 October 2026: it has happened, and without an announcement. Between 19 and 29 September three further Colorado law-enforcement transcripts appeared in the catalogue — LLE-UAP-D002, D003 and D004 — and the video PR004 changed date, from October 2023 to January 2024. The three documents carry a publication date of 24 September on their own pages, but the catalogue files them under the release date of the 18th, so from inside there is no way to tell they arrived late: it shows only against a copy you kept yourself. Tranche six is now 75 items — 59 PDFs, 15 videos and one audio file; 67 from the Pentagon and eight from Colorado — and the series totals 450. The figures above are the ones the catalogue carried on 19 September, and they stand as they were.

What we could not do

Let us be explicit about the limit of this piece, because with the previous tranches we got further.

With tranche five we downloaded all sixteen videos and measured them one by one; that is how we found that five of them carried their coordinates and timestamps covered with fixed black rectangles. This time it took longer: the Department of War portal blocks automated requests, and the archival mirror we use as a fallback was offline for several days.

So what is above is what the official announcement and the specialist press report — not what we have measured. On 1 October we found the route — the department's own uap-data.csv catalogue, recovered from the archive, carries the DVIDS identifiers — and downloaded all fifteen. They have now been measured, and what came out is published separately.

Thirty-seven of the seventy-two files were already public. Does that make the tranche less valuable, or is the problem in how it gets counted? Tell us in the comments.
