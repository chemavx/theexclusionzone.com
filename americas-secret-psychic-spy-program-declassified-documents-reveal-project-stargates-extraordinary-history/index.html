America's Secret Psychic Spy Program: Declassified Documents Reveal Project Stargate's Extraordinary History

Between 1972 and 1995, Project Stargate was the CIA and military's investment in remote viewing as a Cold War intelligence tool. Declassified documents show it employed 15-20 viewers conducting hundreds of operational missions.

ChemaVX

The U.S. government spent $20 million over 23 years training soldiers to see through walls, predict future events, and spy on distant Soviet installations using only their minds — and the declassified evidence proves it actually happened.

Twenty-Three Years of Funded Remote Viewing

Between 1972 and 1995, Project Stargate (operating under various code names including SCANATE, GRILL FLAME, CENTER LANE, and SUN STREAK) represented the American intelligence community's sustained investment in remote viewing — the claimed ability to perceive distant locations, objects, or events using extrasensory perception. The program began quietly at Stanford Research Institute in California and eventually evolved into a military operational unit stationed at Fort Meade, Maryland.

The initiative emerged from Cold War anxiety. In 1972, American intelligence received classified reports suggesting the Soviet Union was investing heavily in psychic research for espionage purposes. Fearing a "psychic gap" equivalent to the famous "missile gap" scare of the 1950s, the CIA approved funding for systematic investigation into whether human consciousness could transcend normal spatial limitations.

Physicists Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ spearheaded the early research at SRI, beginning with civilian volunteers claiming psychic abilities. By the late 1970s, the program expanded to include military personnel, with the Army establishing an operational remote viewing unit. Control subsequently transferred between the CIA, Army Intelligence Command (INSCOM), and the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) throughout its existence.

The Program's Scope and Key Participants

Approximately 12 million pages of declassified documents reveal the scope and ambition of this undertaking. The program employed roughly 15-20 remote viewers at peak capacity, with some participants conducting hundreds of sessions tasking them with describing Soviet military facilities, locating hostages, predicting weapons development, and gathering intelligence on strategic targets.

Joseph McMoneagle, a decorated Army officer who served as "Remote Viewer #001," participated in approximately 450 operational missions between 1978 and 1984. According to CIA assessments, McMoneagle achieved roughly 20% accuracy in his remote viewing sessions — substantially above the 5-7% baseline for random guessing on complex intelligence targets. He received the Legion of Merit upon retirement, unusual recognition for work on a program officially deemed unsuccessful. The gap between that commendation and the official conclusion of failure is one of the program's most revealing details.

Pat Price, a former police commissioner recruited in the early 1970s, allegedly provided remarkably detailed drawings of secret Soviet nuclear research facilities at Semipalatinsk. His sketches reportedly matched classified satellite photography with striking accuracy, including specific dimensions of unusual structures later confirmed to be components of weapons development infrastructure. Price died unexpectedly in 1975 under circumstances that have never been fully explained.

Ingo Swann, an artist and the program's intellectual architect, developed Coordinate Remote Viewing (CRV) — a structured protocol that transformed ad-hoc psychic experimentation into systematic methodology. Before NASA's Pioneer 10 spacecraft revealed Jupiter's faint ring system in 1979, Swann allegedly described these rings during a remote viewing session in 1973, six years before official discovery.

Lyn Buchanan, another program participant who has remained publicly active, trained hundreds of remote viewers after the program's declassification. In June 2026 — the same month the U.S. government released its third tranche of PURSUE declassified UAP files, covered in our full investigation here — Buchanan made headlines claiming to have identified coordinates of alien bases inside mountains worldwide. Those claims are unverified and go well beyond anything in the declassified STARGATE files. The contrast between the documented program and the current claims of its alumni illustrates precisely how a legitimate classified program can become a vector for unverifiable assertions once it enters the public domain.

What the Statistics Actually Show

Statistician Jessica Utts, hired to evaluate the program independently in 1995, concluded that experimental results exceeded what random chance would produce. Some participants demonstrated statistically significant accuracy rates 5-15% above baseline expectations. Psychologist Ray Hyman, conducting the same review, argued that methodological flaws — including inadequate controls for sensory leakage and the file-drawer problem — could explain the modest positive results without invoking paranormal mechanisms.

Operational successes cited in declassified documents include locating a crashed Soviet aircraft in the Democratic Republic of Congo in 1976, allegedly helping identify hidden hostages during international crises, and assisting in the identification of Scud missile positions during the Gulf War. However, official records contain no confirmation that remote viewing intelligence influenced any actual military decisions or operations.

Why the Program Failed Operationally

The apparent contradiction between statistical evidence and operational utility defines the program's fundamental ambiguity. Even the sympathetic evaluation by Utts acknowledged that whatever phenomenon was occurring remained too inconsistent and vague to guide real-world intelligence operations. Beyond the statistical arguments, operational reality posed a devastating problem: even if remote viewers could perceive distant locations slightly better than chance, 15% accuracy means an 85% error rate. No military commander would base strategy on information that's probably wrong.

This tension reflects deeper questions about how science evaluates extraordinary claims — the same questions that surround UAP investigation today. The AARO review of eight decades of UAP research reached a structurally similar conclusion: the government investigated seriously, found results too ambiguous to act on, and officially concluded the evidence was insufficient — while simultaneously classifying the most sensitive data. Whether that pattern reflects genuine absence of extraordinary phenomena or systematic suppression of inconvenient findings is the question that connects STARGATE to every UAP investigation that followed it.

The Cold War Context

The Cold War context provides crucial perspective. Both superpowers operated under genuine fear that their adversary might develop capabilities providing decisive intelligence advantages. This competitive pressure drove investment in unlikely avenues regardless of plausibility. The program persisted not because evidence overwhelmingly supported remote viewing, but because the stakes — if the Soviets succeeded and America failed to investigate — seemed too high to ignore. The same logic, applied to UAP, appears in David Grusch's June 2026 Capitol testimony: the national security argument for continued classification rests not on confirmed extraordinary phenomena but on the risk of adversaries gaining advantage if the U.S. stops investigating, as examined in our full account of the Capitol press conference.

Neither Proven Nor Disproven

Project Stargate remains one of the most thoroughly documented investigations of paranormal phenomena in history, yet it conclusively proved neither that psychic abilities exist nor that they don't. Declassified documents demonstrate that the U.S. government took remote viewing seriously enough to fund it for over two decades with significant resources, yet concluded it lacked practical value for intelligence gathering.

Whether this represents wise investment in unlikely possibilities that ultimately failed, or premature abandonment of genuinely anomalous discoveries, remains disputed. The classified documents themselves cannot resolve this fundamental question — they simply document that from 1972 to 1995, the American intelligence community explored the boundaries of human consciousness and found them far more ambiguous than either believers or skeptics anticipated. Fifty years later, that ambiguity hasn't been resolved. It's just been reclassified under different program names.
