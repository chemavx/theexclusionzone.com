UMMO 1966: Spain's Thirty-Year Alien Correspondence and the Man Who Wrote It

For nearly thirty years, letters from a planet called Ummo convinced educated Spaniards that aliens lived among them. In 1993 the author confessed he had written every page. The story of Spain's largest UFO hoax, and what it teaches.

For nearly thirty years, a growing circle of people in Spain opened their mail to find typewritten letters signed by the inhabitants of a planet called Ummo. The documents ran to hundreds, then thousands of pages. They explained Ummo's social system, its physics, its biology, and its considered assessment of the human species. There were photographs. There was an alleged landing in Madrid. There were intelligent, educated people who believed every word.

And at the end there was a man who admitted he had written all of it himself.

UMMO is the largest UFO hoax in Spanish history, and for exactly that reason it is one of the most instructive files in it. It is a natural experiment in how an elaborate belief gets built, why it holds, and which warning signs were visible from the very first letter — for anyone who was looking.

Madrid, 1966: letters from an impossible sender

The story begins in the Madrid of the mid-sixties, in the circle of enthusiasts of the strange who met regularly in cafés around the capital. From 1966 onward, several people who attended those gatherings began receiving anonymous letters from an implausible correspondent: beings from a planet called Ummo, quietly installed on Earth for years in order to study us.

The letters arrived typewritten, in correct Spanish, and carried the visual signature that would define the case: a symbol of strokes resembling an H crossed by a vertical bar. They were numbered. They cross-referenced each other. And they were written in a peculiar register, half technical report and half diplomatic cable.

The volume grew year after year into a corpus of thousands of pages, ranging from the political structure of Ummo to reflections on physics, mathematics, biology, religion and the destiny of humanity — always in a tone of benevolent superiority.

Context matters here, and English-language accounts usually skip it. This was late-Franco Spain: a country with tight control of the press, limited access to foreign scientific publication, and an intellectual class hungry for material that felt cosmopolitan and unofficial at once. A private correspondence from an advanced civilization, addressed to you personally and to nobody else, landed in that environment with unusual force.

The physical anchor: Aluche and San José de Valderas

On February 6, 1966, several witnesses reported a disc-shaped object sitting in an empty lot in the Madrid district of Aluche. The object displayed, on its side, the same symbol that appeared on the letters.

That episode gave the case something the letters alone could never supply: a physical anchor, witnesses, and a specific place. From then on this stopped being an eccentric correspondence and became a UFO case with a presence in the press.

The next escalation came in 1967, when photographs surfaced of a disc-shaped craft over San José de Valderas, in Alcorcón. The images showed the Ummo symbol on the underside of the craft with real clarity. They were circulated alongside a witness account, and for years a good part of Spanish ufology treated them as some of the best photographic evidence available in the country. They appeared in books, in magazines, in lectures.

The technical analysis, when it came, was devastating. Investigators who studied the negatives concluded that the images were consistent with a small model photographed at close range, not with a large object at middle distance. Ufologist Antonio Moya Cerpa found indications of forgery. And as early as February 1970, investigator Rey Brea wrote to Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos with his conclusion: the photographs had been taken on a tripod, and they were fakes.

Note the date. The photographic evidence was privately known to be worthless in 1970. The corpus kept growing for another two decades.

Why intelligent people believed it

Reducing this case to the gullibility of its followers would be unfair and, more to the point, would explain nothing. The Ummo material was built with considerable intelligence.

First, the tone. The letters promised no salvation and demanded no obedience. They presented themselves as the reports of neutral observers, in sober language full of qualifications, caveats and footnotes. That bureaucratic register was far more credible than any messianic proclamation could have been.

Second, the technical content. The documents included material on physics, cosmology and mathematics with just enough formal apparatus to impress an educated non-specialist. When professional physicists examined them closely they found a mosaic of real concepts badly assembled, conceptual errors, and claims impossible to check. But to a well-read general reader, they scanned as rigorous.

Third, and most effective, the distribution strategy. The documents were not published. They were sent to specific, selected people, who felt themselves chosen recipients of something exceptional. That mechanism of exclusivity produces a psychological commitment that open publication never generates.

The result was that the case captured people of real intellectual and professional standing. The best known was the businessman Rafael Farriols, who funded investigations and co-wrote a book defending the material's authenticity.

1993: the author writes a letter of his own

In 1993, José Luis Jordán Peña wrote to Rafael Farriols to acknowledge that he was the sole author of the hoax. In the years that followed he repeated it publicly in various forums.

Jordán Peña was a technical engineer with an interest in parapsychology and a regular participant in exactly those Madrid gatherings of the sixties. Which is to say: he had been inside the circle from the beginning. He knew the recipients. He knew precisely what kind of material would strike them as credible.

His stated explanation was that he had conceived the whole thing as an experiment in human credulity — he wanted to see how far a belief could be sustained with no foundation underneath it — and that it had eventually outgrown him. He also detailed the technical side: for the photographs he used a model built from household materials, suspended on a fine thread and shot at a fast shutter speed.

The confession did not close the case for everyone. A faction of defenders maintains that Jordán Peña claimed authorship that was not his, or that he was pressured, or that he confessed to part of the hoax in order to cover a genuine phenomenon. None of these hypotheses has produced evidence. All of them share one structural feature: they are immune to refutation.

A recurring line of speculation suggests the case may have been connected to psychological warfare operations, either as an experiment in group manipulation or as cover for something else. The setting — late-Franco Spain, the Cold War, the presence of American bases — makes the speculation attractive.

Let us be clear: there is no documentation supporting it. It is an interesting possibility that keeps being repeated because it fits well narratively, not because a single piece of paper has ever surfaced to sustain it. Treating it as established fact would be committing, in the opposite direction, precisely the error the believers made.

What the content reveals about its author

It is worth pausing on what the documents actually said, because it explains the corpus's appeal better than any psychological analysis.

They described Ummo as a planet orbiting a star the senders called Iumma, identified with Wolf 424 in the constellation Virgo, some fourteen light-years away. They detailed a society organized around a council, with a strongly collectivist value system, extreme social control, and a religiosity built on an abstract entity. The technical sections offered disquisitions on a supposed structure of spacetime with additional dimensions, on the nature of matter, and on comparative biology — always with an abundance of invented terminology and numerical precision.

The Ummites also explained that they had been among us for decades, that they preferred not to reveal themselves openly, and that they shared information in a deliberately incomplete way.

That last feature is the most revealing of all from an analytical standpoint: the corpus contained, built into itself, the justification for why it would never produce conclusive proof. A system that explains its own lack of evidence in advance is designed — consciously or not — to resist verification.

And the letters contained errors that gave the game away. The astronomical descriptions of the Wolf 424 system were contradicted by later observation, and several claims about physics were incompatible with what was already known at the time. A correspondent capable of interstellar travel should not be wrong about his own star.

Three lessons that outlive the case

UMMO is valuable precisely because it was false. It leaves three tests that work on any body of claimed evidence.

Volume is not weight. The Ummo corpus was enormous, and for years the sheer quantity of material was mistaken for evidentiary solidity. But thousands of pages written by one person are worth no more than one page: the amount of documentation proves nothing at all if all of it comes from the same unverifiable source.

Nothing was ever checkable. In almost thirty years, not one element of the Ummo material produced a testable prediction that came true. No anticipated astronomical datum, no verifiable technical development, nothing a reader could independently confirm. It was self-referential from end to end.

Chain of custody decides everything. The photographs arrived with no traceability — no controlled original negative, no reliable identification of the photographer, no way to reconstruct where or when they were taken. That defect alone was enough, in 1967, to assign them minimal evidentiary value. It took Spanish ufology decades to say so out loud.

The same machine, a different country

Readers who know the American files will recognize the architecture immediately. Eighteen years after the first Ummo letter, a 35mm film canister with no return address turned up in a Los Angeles television producer's mailbox and put into circulation the Majestic-12 documents: real names, real dates, real ranks, assembled so that checking the checkable always came back positive. Same construction, same defense mechanisms, same forty-year afterlife. Neither corpus survives contact with chain-of-custody questions, and both are still cited.

There is also a specifically Spanish lesson here, and it is the more hopeful one. The country that produced the largest hoax in UFO history also produced one of the most complete official declassifications: between 1992 and 1999 the Spanish Air Force released its entire UFO archive, and researchers could finally argue from documents with a reference number attached rather than from anonymous letters. The contrast is not a coincidence. Ummo thrived in the absence of an accessible official record. Once there was one, the ground it grew on disappeared.

And Spain offers the third variant too: a real, massive, genuinely witnessed event whose explanation turned out to be prosaic and classified. The 1976 Canary Islands light was seen by over a million people and took eighteen years of declassification to explain. Three cases, three different failure modes: a fabrication, a secret, and an archive. Telling them apart is the whole job.
