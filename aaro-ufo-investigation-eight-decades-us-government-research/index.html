AARO's UFO Investigation: What Eight Decades of U.S. Government Research Actually Found

In March 2024, AARO released the most comprehensive U.S. government review of UAP in history. Eight decades of investigation, over 800 reports—and no evidence of alien technology. Here's what the data actually shows.

On March 8, 2024, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) released a comprehensive review spanning eight decades of U.S. government programs investigating unidentified anomalous phenomena. The result may disappoint conspiracy theorists but reassure scientists: AARO found no evidence that any U.S. government investigation has ever confirmed extraterrestrial technology or that the Department of Defense harbors or reverse-engineers alien spacecraft. This public version of a congressionally mandated report categorically dismisses the most sensational claims about UFOs—including allegations by decorated military officers that the government operates secret crash-recovery programs.

A Bureaucratic Evolution from Secrecy to Science

AARO itself is a relatively new institution, established in July 2022 to consolidate decades of fragmented investigation efforts. It replaced the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF), which operated from 2020 to 2021 within the Office of Naval Intelligence. Before that, the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) ran from 2007 to 2012, representing one of several starts and stops in official U.S. government UAP work.

The term "unidentified anomalous phenomena" itself underwent a semantic shift during NASA's parallel independent study. Congress initially defined UAP as "unidentified aerial phenomena," but it was later expanded to encompass objects observed in air, sea, space, and on land. This terminological evolution reflects a broader scientific maturation in how the government addresses the subject.

Former AARO director Sean Kirkpatrick handed over the office in December 2023 to Jon T. Kosloski, a physicist and engineer with intelligence community experience. Under Kosloski's leadership since August 2024, the office continues receiving reports—over 800 since October 2023—yet maintains its evidence-free position on extraterrestrial origins.

What the Investigation Actually Discovered

The Kona Blue rejection represents perhaps the most revealing finding. AARO uncovered a previously undisclosed proposal submitted to the Department of Homeland Security, designed specifically to reverse-engineer recovered extraterrestrial craft. DHS leadership rejected it for lacking merit, and AARO's investigation confirmed that the program never recovered any otherworldly materials. The report states plainly: no extraterrestrial craft or bodies were ever collected—the material was only assumed to exist by Kona Blue advocates.

Circular reporting invalidates the most dramatic claims. AARO determined that specific allegations involving named individuals, known locations, and documented technological tests related to extraterrestrial reverse-engineering are inaccurate, resulting from circular reporting among a group of individuals who believe this to be the case despite the lack of any evidence. This directly addresses July 2023 Congressional testimony by decorated military veteran David Grusch, who claimed knowledge of a multi-decade UAP crash retrieval program—claims AARO traced back to unverified hearsay rather than firsthand witnesses. Grusch's allegations, and the Congressional response they triggered, will be examined in depth in our next investigation.

Data quality explains most unknowns. AARO has received more than 800 reports since October 2023, yet attributes most unresolved cases to inadequate information rather than genuinely anomalous phenomena. The report emphasizes that the vast majority of reports almost certainly result from misidentification—a direct consequence of insufficient domain awareness. There exists a direct correlation between the quality of available information on a case and the ability to conclusively resolve it.

Prosaic explanations dominate when data exists. Detailed analysis of the "GoFast" video, recorded by Navy aviators from USS Theodore Roosevelt in January 2015, illustrates this clearly. Using metadata embedded in the infrared display, researchers calculated that the object traveled approximately 390 meters in 22 seconds—an average speed of 40 mph, consistent with typical wind speeds at 13,000 feet altitude. The object appeared cold against the warm ocean, suggesting no active propulsion system. What initially seemed like extraordinary velocity was largely an artifact of the sensor platform's own 435-mph groundspeed and parallax effects.

Reporting rates increased when stigma decreased. Between March 2021 and August 2022, the Pentagon received 247 new UAP reports—nearly matching the 263 filed during the entire 17-year period prior to March 2021. This dramatic increase correlates directly with the DoD's decision to encourage rather than discourage military aviators from disclosing anomalies. NASA's involvement further reduced stigma, as the agency's reputation for scientific integrity makes the topic more academically respectable.

What This Means for Science, Policy, and Future Investigation

The AARO findings underscore a fundamental scientific principle: absence of evidence, when the evidence has been rigorously sought, constitutes strong evidence of absence. The report's emphasis on data quality rather than data scarcity reveals that the mystery of UAP is primarily an epistemological problem, not necessarily an ontological one. Most unidentified objects likely represent mundane phenomena—balloons, drones, atmospheric effects—observed under poor conditions with inadequate sensor calibration and metadata.

NASA's independent study team, which released its own report in September 2023, reached parallel conclusions and went further in prescribing solutions. The panel determined that AI and machine learning cannot effectively identify anomalies without baseline data of sufficient quality. Rather than developing new analysis techniques, the priority should be improving data acquisition, calibration, and curation—a significant reorientation of investigative focus.

Physical constraints derived from known aviation capabilities provide the strongest investigative tool. State-of-the-art military aircraft, drones, and balloons operate within established velocity and acceleration ranges. Any credible observation of objects exceeding these parameters—verified through multiple, calibrated sensors with comprehensive metadata—would constitute scientifically interesting evidence. Conversely, if all reported UAP exhibit conventional velocities and accelerations, conventional explanations become overwhelmingly probable.

Eight Decades Later, the Bottleneck Is Data

The AARO report and NASA's independent study converge on a single conclusion: the UAP mystery persists largely because investigation efforts have historically been fragmented, stigmatized, and poorly instrumented. Eight decades of government programs produced numerous anecdotes but few systematic datasets. The path forward demands not exotic theories but methodical science—better sensors, rigorous calibration, comprehensive metadata, and transparent data curation.

That institutional commitment is now being tested in real time. In May 2026, the U.S. government declassified 222 UAP files spanning eight decades—the most comprehensive disclosure in American history—yet the Pentagon maintains that none of the materials confirm extraterrestrial life or recovered alien technology. The AARO framework, built on evidence standards and data quality, is precisely the lens through which those 222 files must be read. On June 9, Congressional figures and whistleblower David Grusch will hold a press conference at the Capitol pushing for deeper transparency. Whether that pressure produces new evidence or new anecdotes will determine whether the next eight decades of UAP investigation look any different from the last.
