The June 2026 Triangle: Two Files on One Night, and the Line Everyone Is Quoting Is in Neither

The widely quoted line that AARO wants the June 2026 triangle investigated is not in the memorandum. We compared AARO's memo with the FBI's FD-302 and its nine photographs.
Share
ChemaVX

One line from the seventh PURSUE release has been quoted everywhere this week: that AARO considers the June 2026 triangle case to warrant further investigation, because its morphology and kinematics line up with other incidents in the western United States. It is a striking sentence, and it is being attributed to the Pentagon's anomaly office.

It is not in the memorandum. We searched all three pages: the words "warrant", "further investigation", "assess", "conclusion" and "recommend" do not appear. The sentence lives in the catalogue blurb — the descriptive text the Department of War writes beside each file on its own website. It is the publishing department's characterisation, not a finding recorded by the investigators inside the document.

That matters, because the memorandum says the opposite about itself. Twice it warns that the report "reflects the reporter's interpretation at the time of the event" and that such characterisations "should not be interpreted as a conclusive indication of the presence or absence of any intrinsic object features or performance characteristics". The FBI's file carries its own boilerplate: this document contains neither recommendations nor conclusions of the FBI.

Two agencies, one night

The release contains two separate files on the same incident. DOW-UAP-D176 is a three-page memorandum for record from the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security, documenting AARO's witness debrief. FBI-UAP-D043 is an FD-302, the Bureau's interview record form, running to eleven pages — two of text and nine of photographs.

The witnesses are the same in both: two Federal Special Agents, with a third observer at a separate post corroborating the event. Names are withheld in both under codes 1843(a)(2) and 1843(a)(3). The catalogue places it in June 2026, in the western United States.

Worth noting: the catalogue blurb also un-redacts things the document hides. It supplies the June date the memorandum blacks out, calls the location "a sensitive national security site" where the memo says only "observation post", and refers to a second sighting the following evening that does not appear in the three published pages.

Where the two accounts diverge

The shared narrative: at about 2023 local, a third observer at another post calls out a thermally elevated object to the west. Two agents move across their position and acquire it with the naked eye, silhouetted against the last of the western sky. It is dark, matte, non-reflective and triangular. It climbs vertically over a ridge without tilting or wobbling, stops, holds station, then goes from a standstill into fast horizontal flight, banks, and descends terrain-hugging into a canyon. It makes no sound at any point. In infrared it shows a uniform thermal signature across the whole airframe, with no hot spots and no exhaust plume. It has no intakes, control surfaces, windows or canopy. A ground search of the canyon the next morning found no wreckage, scarring or damaged vegetation.

The numbers do not match between the two files:

  • Wingspan: AARO estimates thirteen to fifteen feet; the FD-302 says nine to fifteen.
  • Range: AARO brackets it between 1.4 and 3.2 miles using terrain cross-reference; the FBI gives about 3.2 miles, and in the second statement roughly 1.5.
  • Height above the ridge: AARO says 200 to 300 feet; the FD-302, about 200.
  • Thermal signature: both describe it as uniform, but the FBI adds something AARO does not emphasise — it appeared hotter than ambient temperature.
  • Duration: AARO breaks the event into three phases — about ten seconds of vertical ascent, about twenty of station-keeping and two to three of final manoeuvre — adding up to a little over half a minute; the FD-302 says the total duration of the sighting was approximately three seconds.

There is also a difference in vocabulary that is not accidental. AARO's memorandum says "the unidentified craft" throughout. The FBI's agents compare it to "a delta wing drone" and, closing the statement, call it plainly "the unmanned aerial system". The men who watched it were filing it as a machine.

The nine photographs

The part of the FBI file that has not been reported is the imagery. The last nine pages of the FD-302 are large colour photographs — up to 5,678 by 3,791 pixels — shot from a fixed point: the framing is identical in all of them, same valley, same ridge, same moon in the same place. Each shows a dark elongated object against the hillside, in a different position.

We measured the movement by comparing each frame against the median of all nine, which reconstructs the background without the object. The result is a clean, continuous track: the object climbs through the first four frames, peaks on the fourth, and descends across the remaining five, drifting about 590 pixels leftward across the frame as it goes. The curve reproduces, with nobody interpreting anything, what both written accounts describe: ascent, hold, and a fall toward the canyon.

What that measurement does not establish is size, range or speed. Without the true distance to the object and the camera's optical parameters, pixels do not convert into feet or knots. Anyone publishing a speed figure derived from these images is making it up.

One further limit, worth stating plainly: the photographs carry no metadata at all. No timestamp, no camera model, no coordinates — zero EXIF fields. That may be a consequence of embedding them in the PDF, or a deliberate scrub before release; the file does not let you tell which. Either way, the time, the place and the device cannot be verified from the document. You have to take the text's word for it.

What actually stands

Two federal agencies separately documented the same episode, with the same witnesses, in accounts that agree on substance and disagree on numbers — which is what two honest interviews about an event that was over in seconds tend to look like — and they do not even agree on how many. Nine photographs show a sequence consistent with the written narrative. And neither file contains a single sentence asserting what the object was.

The Department of War does say, in its catalogue entry, that the case warrants further investigation. That carries weight — it comes from the custodian of the archive — but it is not the same thing as a finding by the investigators, and it should be quoted as what it is.

The seventh release holds twenty-six other documents, nine of them from the DIA's AAWSAP contract, one of which we have already worked through: the Utah ranch field report. From earlier releases we have closed out the count for Release 6 and the forensic analysis of its fifteen videos.
