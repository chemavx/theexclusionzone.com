222 Declassified UFO Files: What They Reveal, What They Hide and What Remains Unanswered

Two releases, 222 files from WWII to 2026. We break down what's actually in the Pentagon's documents, what contradictions they contain and what critical questions remain completely unanswered.
Share
ChemaVX

On May 8, 2026, the U.S. Department of War launched the most comprehensive government disclosure of unidentified aerial phenomena records in American history, making 158 previously classified records publicly accessible without security clearance requirements — 162 individual files, since a single record can bundle several. Within two weeks, a second tranche added 64 more records, bringing the total to 222 declassified records spanning eight decades—from 1944 World War II sightings to 2026 military reports. The revelation has already generated over 1 billion website visits and reignited global debate about what the government knows regarding unexplained objects observed by military pilots, astronauts, and defense personnel.

Years of Congressional Pressure and Presidential Directives

The declassification initiative traces its origins to bipartisan Congressional pressure extending back years. The U.S. Department of Defense formally established the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) in July 2022 specifically to investigate unexplained aerial phenomena across air, maritime, space, and transmedium domains. This office became the central authority coordinating investigations across the Pentagon and intelligence community.

In February 2026, President Donald Trump ordered federal agencies to identify and declassify all records connected to unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP), extraterrestrial life, and unidentified flying objects (UFOs). The directive emerged days after former President Barack Obama stated in a podcast interview that aliens were "real" but clarified he saw no evidence during his presidency. Trump publicly accused Obama of disclosing classified information and demanded his administration prove the government had been withholding secrets about space visitors.

The legal framework supporting this massive disclosure includes the James M. Inhofe National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023, which codified UAP reporting procedures and created whistleblower protections for government employees providing classified information about UAP programs. Congress previously mandated that records must undergo periodic declassification review, with public disclosure occurring no later than 25 years after creation. The 2024 National Defense Authorization Act established Record Group 615 at the National Archives specifically to house unidentified anomalous phenomena records transferred from federal agencies.

The PURSUE System and First Release: May 8, 2026

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced the Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters (PURSUE), a dedicated government website created to centralize declassified materials at war.gov/ufo. The initial release deployed 162 files containing 120 PDFs, 28 videos totaling 41 minutes of footage, and 14 image files drawn from the FBI, Department of War, NASA, and State Department archives.

Secretary Hegseth stated the goal was providing "maximum transparency" about the government's knowledge of unexplained phenomena. Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard described a "comprehensive" multi-agency declassification program underway, while FBI Director Kash Patel emphasized the "level of transparency that no prior administration has ever delivered." NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman applauded the effort, committing the agency to remain "candid about what we know to be true, what we have yet to understand, and all that remains to be discovered."

The files date from 1944 to the present, though gaps exist in coverage from 1974–1984 and 2005–2012. Geographic distribution spans 34 locations across six continents, the Moon, and low Earth orbit, with concentrations in the Middle East where U.S. military maintains substantial presence and sophisticated monitoring capabilities.

Apollo Astronaut Testimony: Lunar Observations Never Before Public

The declassified materials include previously restricted NASA transcripts from Apollo missions, marking the first public release of certain astronaut accounts. Buzz Aldrin, astronaut aboard Apollo 11, described in a 1969 debriefing seeing "little flashes inside the cabin, spaced a couple of minutes apart," and reported observing "what appeared to be a fairly bright light source which we tentatively ascribed to a possible laser."

Apollo 12 astronaut Alan Bean reported witnessing "flashes of light" described as "sailing off into space" during the 1969 mission, with particles appearing as though "escaping the Moon." Apollo 17 astronauts in 1972 reported flashing lights near the lunar surface, with Jack Schmitt exclaiming, "It looks like the Fourth of July out there!" in reference to American fireworks displays. A photograph from Apollo 17 shows three dots in triangular formation in the lower right quadrant of the lunar sky. The Pentagon noted that new preliminary analysis has been initiated on this Apollo 17 image, indicating ongoing investigation.

Frank Borman's audio transcript from the Gemini 7 mission (December 1965) captures live air-to-ground communication where he reports a "bogey" to Houston and describes seeing "trillions of little particles" at approximately four miles distance. This represents the only audio/video recording from the first release capturing real-time spacecraft observations.

Military Engagement Footage: First-Ever Released Shootdown Video

The May 22 second release added 64 files, including footage that Pentagon officials describe as unprecedented. A video from Lake Huron dated February 12, 2023, shows the first-ever publicly released footage of a U.S. military engaging an unidentified aerial object. F-16 infrared video captures an AIM-9X Sidewinder missile engagement, with the object appearing balloon-like on thermal imaging before dispersing upon impact. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office assessed the video as "likely derived from an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform."

A 2022 submarine video shows spherical objects repeatedly entering and exiting water near a submarine, exhibiting transmedium behavior—seamless air-to-water transition—described as one of "Five Observables" that defies conventional physics. This capability represents a flight characteristic not attributed to any known aerial platform.

Military sensor videos now total 75 across CENTCOM, INDOPACOM, AFRICOM, and domestic operations. A video from Syria in 2023 shows an object sustaining approximately 424 knots (483 mph) for at least seven minutes, described as "shaped as a bouncy ball." Another video from Greece in October 2023 documents an object making multiple 90-degree turns at approximately 80 miles per hour while flying just above the ocean surface—maneuvers assessed as aerodynamically impossible for conventional aircraft at that speed.

Cold War Era Investigations: New Mexico Green Orbs and Copper Residue

The second release includes 116 pages documenting 209 sightings of "green orbs," "discs," and "fireballs" near Sandia, New Mexico, between 1948 and 1950—the most extensive Cold War UAP file ever released. Multiple sites where copper powder residue was found accompany witness reports. These cases fed into Project Grudge, the 1949 U.S. Air Force program investigating unexplained aerial phenomena.

A late 2025 intelligence officer report describes two large orange oval orbs with white or yellow centers spotted near a helicopter, subsequently observed "chasing" fighter jets in coordinated formation before disappearing. Multiple trained military personnel observed the objects forming a distinct triangle pattern. This incident exemplifies formation flight and coordinated behavior among multiple objects tracked from 2020–2023 in the Arabian Gulf, suggesting intelligent coordination between separate objects.

Pentagon Position: No Confirmation of Extraterrestrial Life

Despite extensive documentation spanning decades, the Pentagon explicitly stated that the released materials do not confirm extraterrestrial life or recovered alien technology. The Department of War described files as "unresolved cases, meaning the government is unable to make a definitive determination on the nature of the observed phenomena." The 2024 Pentagon report formally disclaimed possession of information pertaining to extraterrestrial life existence or recovery of alien technology.

The Pentagon website includes a disclaimer that "descriptive and estimative language" in military memos reflects "subjective interpretation" of report authors and should not be interpreted as conclusive indication of what actually occurred. Many cases remain unresolved due to limited data or unclear imagery, with several photos and videos not yet fully analyzed for definitive attribution.

Scientific and Skeptical Reception: Optical Artifacts and Camera Distortions

Scientific reaction to the disclosures has been mixed. David Whitehouse, astrophysicist and former BBC science journalist, reviewed the materials and concluded "some are optical artefacts, others fuzzy blobs, and some light smears. Some obviously balloons. No hint, no evidence whatsoever of anything artificial and alien."

Michael Narlock, astronomer at the Cranbrook Institute of Science, stated the documents largely contained eyewitness account transcripts that are "notoriously unreliable," while videos lacked sufficient context for assessment. Seth Shostak, senior astronomer at the SETI Institute, reaffirmed that "there is no compelling evidence for extraterrestrial life thus far." Sean M. Kirkpatrick, former AARO director and physicist, stated there was nothing unexpected in the release.

Writer Mick West declared there was "nothing really interesting" in the first release. New York Post video editor Steven Greenstreet noted that some contents had been in the public record for years already and claimed they were previously discredited as possible camera artifacts. Writer Jason Colavito observed that "most of the UFO material released … had been made public decades ago," though he acknowledged appreciation for high-quality color scans improving accessibility compared to previous black-and-white Xerox copies.

Information Decontextualization and Conspiracy Theory Amplification

Critics warned that presenting unresolved materials without definitive conclusions creates information gaps that conspiracy narratives exploit. In Catalonia, the Center for Opinion Studies found that 20% of the population believes governments hide evidence of extraterrestrial contact—a belief reinforced by official messaging about previously hidden information now made public.

Experts note that confusion between "unidentified phenomena" and "extraterrestrial visitation" represents a logical leap the desinformation ecosystem actively exploits. While science accepts its limitations and uses rigorous methodology to address knowledge gaps, conspiracy narratives prefer sensationalist answers. The Pentagon's admission that certain objects cannot be catalogued definitively is not confirmation of alien life, yet the public distinction between these positions remains muddled in popular discourse.

Congressional and Political Reactions: Demands for More Evidence

Republican Representative Anna Paulina Luna formally requested publication of 46 additional UFO videos identified by whistleblowers. Republican Congressman Tim Burchett, a vocal transparency advocate, thanked President Trump for "keeping his word," calling the release a "great start," while cautioning that "transparency won't happen overnight, it'll take time." He predicted subsequent releases would be more significant than the initial batch.

It was. The second declassification arrived in May with Apollo 12 astronaut testimony and Sandia's Cold War records — a different class of material from this first batch, and the reason the two releases are worth reading apart rather than as one story.

Former Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene criticized the disclosure as a distraction, posting on X that "unless they roll out live aliens and test demo UFOs or actually admit what we know this really is, then I have way better things to do on this Friday." She characterized the effort as "look at the shiny object propaganda" amid ongoing inflation and military conflicts.

Planned Expansion: Second Release and Future Tranches

Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell announced May 18 that additional documents were "actively being processed for publication" with "more to come very soon." The second PURSUE release on May 22 validated this commitment, adding 64 files to the archive. Trump shared AI-generated images depicting himself alongside a handcuffed alien at a military base and piloting a spaceship with other vessels exploding in background—visual content underscoring the entertainment value Trump attributes to the disclosure effort.

The Pentagon committed to rolling releases every few weeks as documents complete declassification review. The National Archives has established formal procedures for receiving UAP records from federal agencies on ongoing basis, making materials available through the National Archives Catalog as they arrive. Defense officials acknowledged that tens of millions of records spanning decades require review, with many existing only in printed format, necessitating extended processing timelines.

Religious Community Concerns: Reports of Strategic Preparation

Multiple Christian pastors claimed U.S. intelligence officials conducted meetings requesting churches prepare congregations for the file release's potential religious implications. Evangelist Perry Stone warned two days before the first release that UAP files "could shatter Christian faith," cautioning that materials might include extraterrestrial aircraft footage. Unnamed sources suggested forthcoming June 2026 releases would reveal "translucent beings" and biological entities, claims unverified by Pentagon officials.

The First Multi-Agency Disclosure — and What It Left Open

The Trump administration's declassification of 222 UAP files represents the first coordinated multi-agency disclosure in U.S. history, providing unprecedented public access to government records previously restricted for decades. While the Pentagon maintains no evidence confirms extraterrestrial life or alien technology recovery, the archive's sheer volume—75 military sensor videos, Apollo astronaut testimony, Cold War investigations, and submarine transmedium footage—demonstrates consistent government documentation of unexplained aerial phenomena across eight decades. The initiative's outcome depends fundamentally on whether public interpretation of ambiguous, unresolved cases advances scientific understanding or amplifies conspiracy narratives exploiting information gaps the government itself cannot definitively explain.
