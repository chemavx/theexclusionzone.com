The Vatican and Extraterrestrials: The Institution That Already Answered

The Vatican has run an observatory since 1582, staffs it with ten Jesuit astrophysicists, and published its position on extraterrestrial life in its own newspaper. While Washington answers in redactions, Rome answered the harder question in print.

While Congress argues about what disclosure would look like, one institution has already had the argument. It settled the theology, published the conclusions, and staffed an observatory to do the astronomy — and it has been at it longer than the United States has existed.

There is no Catholic dogma on extraterrestrial life. What there is, which is arguably more useful, is a century of Jesuit astronomers, theologians and several popes working through the question in public, while the popular assumption remains that the Church and the search for alien life must be natural enemies.

An Observatory Older Than the Telescope Debate

The Vatican Observatory — the Specola Vaticana — traces to 1582. Leo XIII reorganised it formally in 1891 with an explicit purpose: to demonstrate that the Church "does not oppose true and solid science." It operates today from two sites, Castel Gandolfo near Rome and the University of Arizona in Tucson.

Ten astrophysicists, all Jesuit priests or religious, publish in peer-reviewed journals and appear at international conferences on exoplanets and astrobiology. It is not ornamental science. It is funded research, under named authorship, paid for by the oldest institution in the West.

"Brother Extraterrestrial"

In 2008 Father José Gabriel Funes, then the observatory's director, gave an interview to L'Osservatore Romano — the Vatican's own newspaper — under a headline few expected from that masthead: "The Extraterrestrial Is My Brother."

Funes was direct: "I see no difficulty for the Catholic faith." His reasoning started from scale — hundreds of billions of galaxies, each with billions of stars. If that many potentially habitable worlds exist, he asked, how could one exclude that life developed elsewhere too?

His theological argument is the more interesting half. To restrict divine creation to Earth alone would place limits on God's creative freedom, which Catholic doctrine rejects outright. He invoked Francis of Assisi, who called every earthly creature brother and sister, and asked why that fraternity should stop at the atmosphere.

On redemption he speculated that such beings might have no need of it — that they may never have fallen, remaining in full communion with their creator, while terrestrial humanity was, in the parable's terms, the lost sheep that needed retrieving.

The Pope and the Martians

On 12 May 2014, at a morning Mass, Pope Francis offered a hypothetical that travelled further than its context: if an expedition of Martians arrived — green, long-nosed, big-eared, as children draw them — and one asked for baptism, "who are we to close doors?"

It was not, as many headlines had it, a statement about whether extraterrestrials exist. It was a rhetorical device about the radical inclusivity of the Gospel, framed by Peter's vision in Acts, and its real subject was the Church's openness to those regarded as outsiders — with direct application to migrants.

But the underlying question was legitimate, and the implied answer was unambiguous: if a rational extraterrestrial being asked for baptism, there is no doctrinal impediment.

The Book With the Unambiguous Title

Brother Guy Consolmagno, the Jesuit astronomer who has directed the observatory since 2015, took the reflection further. In 2018 he published, with Father Paul Mueller, a book called Would You Baptize an Extraterrestrial?

His answer is yes, if the being asked knowingly. That is not a claim about alien life existing. It is the observation that Catholic doctrine contains no prohibition.

Consolmagno also rejects the assumption that such a discovery would trigger a crisis of faith. He expects the public would meet it with curiosity rather than theological panic — a prediction worth noting precisely because it contradicts the premise of most disclosure discourse, which assumes societal shock as a given and sometimes uses it to justify withholding.

Thirty Scientists, at the Vatican, on Astrobiology

In November 2009 the Pontifical Academy of Sciences convened its first Study Week on Astrobiology, bringing together thirty scientists from American, French, British, Swiss, Italian and Chilean institutions — astronomers, biologists, geologists, physicists.

Chris Impey of the University of Arizona argued there were plausibly hundreds of millions of habitable locations in the Milky Way alone. The French astronomer Athena Coustenis pointed to Europa, Titan and Enceladus as candidates for microbial life.

The conference's stated conclusion was explicit: the existence of extraterrestrial life does not contradict Catholic faith. Not a binding dogmatic declaration, but a public institutional acknowledgment of a position its astronomers and theologians had held privately for years.

The Argument That Is Still Open

Within contemporary Catholic theology two positions contend over what confirmed intelligent extraterrestrial life would mean.

The first, held by Funes and Consolmagno, is inclusivist: any rational being forms part of divine creation, and if it never fell, it needs no redemption in the traditional sense. If it did fall, Christ's sacrifice might be read as universal, leaning on the passage in Colossians about all created things being made through him and for him.

The second emphasises the historical singularity of the Incarnation. The German theologian Medard Kehl proposed in 2020 that fallen extraterrestrials might have their own redemptive history, entirely independent of ours.

The Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith considered the matter premature for a binding statement in 2024. That is not evasion so much as the same magisterial caution the Church has applied before pronouncing on other unsettled scientific questions.

From Galileo to ExoMars

The history matters here, and not as decoration. In 1633 the Church condemned Galileo. In 1992 John Paul II formally called that judgment an error born of tragic mutual incomprehension. The Belgian priest Georges Lemaître proposed what became the Big Bang in 1927, a theory the Vatican came to embrace. The same observatory now collaborates with international missions including ExoMars.

The institution that condemned Galileo is the institution that funds front-line astrophysics. That trajectory took four centuries and was not graceful, but it happened, and it is the reason the current openness is more than a press line.

Why This Is Worth Noting Now

Set against the eight decades of US government research we have covered, and the arguments now reaching Congress, the contrast is instructive rather than devotional.

One institution has spent decades being asked what it knows and answering in redactions — the PURSUE releases are the current instalment. The other was asked a harder question, about meaning rather than evidence, and answered it in print, under named authorship, in its own newspaper, with the reasoning shown.

Neither tells us whether anything is out there. But only one of them has published what it would think if there were.
