Roswell's Viral "Crater" Video: A Frame-by-Frame Forensic Analysis

Seventy-nine years after Roswell, we ran the National Archives' own 341.ROSWELL.41 tape through a full forensic pipeline: 1,305 frames, Error Level Analysis, noise signatures, perceptual hashing. The viral "crater with an alien body" is a camera filming a photograph in a book.
Share
ChemaVX

Seventy-nine years ago this week, the Roswell Daily Record ran the most famous front page in UFO history: "RAAF Captures Flying Saucer On Ranch in Roswell Region." Within twenty-four hours the story had been retracted, and the argument over what actually fell on the Foster ranch in July 1947 has never stopped.

In September 2025, a new chapter opened when a 21-minute reel quietly appeared in the U.S. National Archives catalog. Social media seized on its closing shots: a desert crater strewn with debris, and in the final frames, a dark vertical shape that spread across platforms as "the alien body in the Roswell crash footage." UFO analyst Mark Lee told the Daily Mail he suspected a hoax and pareidolia — an eyeballed judgment, reasonable but unverified. We wanted to test it properly.

So for this anniversary we went to the primary source instead: item 341.ROSWELL.41, the actual tape held by the National Archives, and ran it through a full forensic pipeline — frame extraction at one-second intervals, automated scene detection, Error Level Analysis, per-segment noise signatures, GOP and bitrate structure, spatial frequency analysis, and perceptual hashing of key frames. Here is what the tape contains, what it doesn't, and why both matter.

What the file actually is

The technical fingerprint tells the story of its provenance before a single frame does. The video is 720×480 anamorphic NTSC — the shape of a broadcast tape, not a camera file — re-encoded to H.264 in June 2023 with a Mainconcept encoder, the toolchain used in professional digitization workflows. The opening seconds carry the National Archives watermark and a title card identifying the source as "Digital Betacam (I Copy)." Whatever is on this file has passed through at least three generations: original film or tape, a Betacam archival master, and the 2023 digital transfer. That chain matters for everything that follows, because each generation destroys some kinds of forensic evidence and leaves fingerprints of its own — and it means any judgment about this tape has to be made about a third-generation copy, not the original capture.

What's on the tape

The footage divides cleanly into two blocks. The first fourteen minutes are 1990s studio B-roll: slow pans over the cover and pages of the Air Force's own Roswell report, interview transcripts, and a stack of the paperbacks that built the modern Roswell canon — Randle and Schmitt's UFO Crash at Roswell and The Truth About the UFO Crash at Roswell, Berlitz and Moore's The Roswell Incident. There is a lingering shot of the July 8, 1947 Roswell Daily Record front page, and — more telling — the December 1960 issue of National Geographic, open to Joseph Kittinger's "The Long, Lonely Leap," his account of the Excelsior high-altitude parachute jumps.

The final seven minutes are the payoff: black-and-white archival film of U.S. Air Force high-altitude balloon operations. A polished instrument gondola stenciled "J.H.U. ASTROPHYSICS V — U.S. AIR FORCE." Night operations with a crane and a cylindrical payload. Daylight suspension tests of a gondola dangling from a crane boom over scrub desert, hoses trailing to the ground, dust kicked up below, a row of support trailers on the horizon. A stratospheric balloon launch.

If that inventory sounds familiar, it should. It is, almost item for item, the evidentiary spine of the Air Force's 1997 report Case Closed: the argument that the 1947 debris came from Project Mogul balloon trains, and that later "body" reports drew on anthropomorphic dummy drops from programs like High Dive and Excelsior — the very jumps in that National Geographic on the desk.

The crater that went viral

We ran the full battery on the closing sequence — minutes 20:20 to 21:23 — and the geometry of the footage answers the question before any pixel-level analysis does.

The "crater pan" is a rostrum move. Affine registration between the first and last frames of the sequence returns a 2.1% scale change and a 46-pixel vertical drift: the signature of a camera panning across a static piece of artwork on a copy stand, not of a camera moving through a location. The sequence that follows shows the same image as a bordered plate with a caption line beneath it — a figure mounted on the page of a book or report, being filmed. Then a page turns, on camera, and the final shot holds on the full-frame version of the plate for nineteen seconds, static to within 2.6 pixels.

The "alien"? It shares the exact film-grain layer of the rest of the plate — noise σ of 7.78 inside the shape against 7.65 outside, a ratio of 1.02 — and its centroid never moves. It is part of the printed photograph. A digital insertion would carry its own noise signature with detectable edges. This one doesn't, because nothing was inserted: someone pointed a film camera at a picture in a book.

We tried to recover the plate's caption to identify the source figure, and here honesty beats a good story: in a third-generation SD transfer, the text sits below the resolution floor. We tested multiple crop regions, denoising filters, and OCR passes — all of them return speckle, not text. We can tell you what the image demonstrably is: a filmed reproduction of a printed still, sitting at the end of a reel that spends fourteen minutes filming books. We cannot yet tell you which publication it came from.

There is a second, quieter version of the same lesson earlier in the tape. In the balloon-test sequence, the widest "site" shot isn't a landscape either — the camera is filming a photographic print held up by hand, the operator's fingers visible on both edges of the frame. Twice in one reel: a photograph of a photograph, compressed a few more generations on its way through the internet, becomes "leaked crash site footage." Nothing needs to be faked for that to happen. Resolution loss does the work on its own.

Is the tape itself manipulated?

We tested for it directly. Error Level Analysis across the terrain frames came back flat and uniform — mean residuals of 0.36 to 0.45 with no localized hotspots of the kind that splicing or cloning leaves behind. The noise floor behaves exactly like photochemical film grain modulated by scene texture, not like a digital composite, where inserted regions carry a different noise signature with hard edges. The spatial frequency spectrum shows the radial falloff of film grain plus telecine scanline harmonics, and nothing else. The compression layer — a uniform GOP structure and a single encoder signature across all 21 minutes — confirms one digital generation with no codec-level splices.

What the tape does show is 24 hard cuts and, at roughly the 12:34 mark, a frame that is half black, half vertical gray striping: a physical tape splice or blanking gap in the Betacam master, not a digital artifact. The grain signature also swings hard between segments — roughly σ 4 in the studio B-roll, over σ 10 in the oldest film transfers. Both are exactly what a compilation reel should look like: multiple sources of different ages, edited together on one archival master, decades before the file ever touched a computer that could alter anything.

Verdict: no indication of digital manipulation. Every anomaly in the file is accounted for by the film → Betacam → H.264 chain.

What independent observers already suspected

We weren't the only ones who doubted the "crash site" reading. When the tape went viral in September 2025, declassified-imagery analyst Mark Lee told the Daily Mail he believed the footage was likely a hoax or repurposed stock material, and called the "alien" shape a case of pareidolia — the tendency to see meaningful patterns, like faces or figures, in random visual noise. Some commenters on the original threads independently guessed the crater sequence was "B-roll for that Roswell presentation the government put together" in the 1990s.

Both of those instincts were right. What this analysis adds is the part that instinct can't provide: measured proof. Affine registration numbers instead of "it looks like a pan." A noise-ratio comparison instead of "it looks pasted in." An ELA map instead of "it looks edited." The conclusion other observers reached by eye, this analysis reaches by pixel-level measurement — and the measurements agree.

What this does and doesn't prove

Let's be precise about the boundaries. This analysis authenticates a tape, not the events of July 1947. Item 341.ROSWELL.41 is the Air Force's own supporting material for its own explanation, and its selection of contents reflects that: it was assembled to make the balloon-and-dummies case. Analyzing it cannot tell you whether that case is complete — the persistent gaps in the Roswell record, including the destroyed outgoing messages of the Roswell Army Air Field from that period, sit entirely outside this file.

That distinction matters because the official explanation itself remains contested by credible voices, not just tabloid enthusiasts. David Grusch, the intelligence officer whose 2023 congressional testimony and 2026 Capitol appearance we've covered extensively, has called the Air Force's official Roswell explanation "a total hack job" and stated under oath that multiple eyewitnesses reported recovered bodies from the incident. We take that testimony seriously elsewhere on this site. It has no bearing on this analysis. Authenticating a support tape and evaluating a 79-year-old eyewitness dispute are different questions, answered with different tools, and this piece only answers the first one.

What we can tell you with confidence: the tape contains no location footage of any impact site. The viral "crater" and "body" frames are a filmed reproduction of a printed photograph — a plate in a book, caption and all — and neither the plate nor the tape around it shows any trace of digital manipulation. It contains balloon hardware, a crane, a famous newspaper, a magazine about a man falling from the stratosphere, and pictures of pictures. Anyone presenting its closing frames as crash-site footage is either mistaken or counting on you not to check.

The same test travels well beyond Roswell. The Saharan rock paintings of Tassili n'Ajjer are read by the ancient-astronaut literature as helmeted visitors in pressure suits, and the figures the whole argument rests on turn out to have been catalogued, named and dated by the archaeologists who found them.

One piece of the puzzle remains open, and we're naming it precisely so it doesn't get lost: we do not know which book or report the viral plate was photographed from. The caption is real, legible in principle, and currently unreadable only because of generational video loss — not because it doesn't exist. If a reader with access to reverse image search tools, the original camera negative, or a copy of The Roswell Incident, UFO Crash at Roswell, or the 1995 and 1997 Air Force reports recognizes this image, that identification would close the last open question in this analysis: who took the photograph, when, and why it ended up filmed for fourteen seconds at the end of an Air Force reel.

The Methodology, Open for Anyone to Rerun

We checked what the internet only glanced at. The methodology — frame inventory, cut list, ELA maps, noise profiles, and perceptual hashes of the key frames — stands ready for anyone who wants to run their own comparisons or push the last question further. Primary sources beat screenshots of screenshots, even when the primary source turns out to be, itself, a screenshot of something else entirely.

341.ROSWELL.41 is a public domain record of the U.S. National Archives, Record Group 341. All frames referenced here are drawn from that record.
