JAL 1628: The Best-Documented UFO Encounter in Aviation History

On November 17, 1986, Japan Airlines Flight 1628 encountered unidentified objects over Alaska for 50 minutes, tracked simultaneously by onboard radar, air traffic control, and military radar. It remains one of the most thoroughly documented UFO cases in aviation history.

On November 17, 1986, a Boeing 747 cargo jet carrying French wine crossed into Alaskan airspace and encountered something that would confound aviation experts, government officials, and skeptics for decades to come — a 50-minute encounter with unidentified objects tracked simultaneously on civilian radar, military radar, and the aircraft's own instruments.

The Flight and the First Sighting

Japan Air Lines Cargo Flight 1628 departed Paris on November 16, 1986, carrying a shipment of Beaujolais wine bound for Tokyo via Anchorage. The crew consisted of three experienced professionals: Captain Kenju Terauchi, a 47-year-old ex-fighter pilot with 29 years of flying experience and over 10,000 flight hours; First Officer Takanori Tamefuji; and Flight Engineer Yoshio Tsukuba. After a scheduled stop in Reykjavik, Iceland, the 747 resumed its trans-polar route at 35,000 feet, cruising at approximately 600 miles per hour.

At 5:11 PM Alaska Standard Time on November 17, near Fort Yukon in the remote northeastern interior of Alaska, Captain Terauchi first noticed two amber-colored lights to his left, approximately 2,000 feet below the aircraft. Initially, he assumed they were military jets from nearby Eielson or Elmendorf Air Force Base on routine patrol. However, after several minutes, he realized the lights were maintaining perfect pace with his aircraft — an unusual behavior that prompted him to contact Anchorage Air Route Traffic Control Center.

The controller's response was immediate: no known traffic in that area. Yet Terauchi reported seeing two objects approximately one mile ahead, displaying white and yellow strobes. What started as a routine inquiry would escalate into one of the most documented aviation UFO encounters in history.

Maneuvers, Lights, and Physical Effects

Over the next several minutes, the encounter intensified dramatically. Two distinct objects with multiple lights arranged in rectangular arrays — resembling exhaust ports or rocket nozzles — began maneuvering around the 747 with extraordinary agility. Terauchi described their movements as defying the laws of physics: they could accelerate instantaneously, stop on a dime, and change direction without apparent deceleration. In his later written statement, he remarked: "The thing was flying as if there was no such thing as gravity. It sped up, then stopped, then flew at our speed, in our direction, so that to us it appeared to be standing still. The next instant it changed course."

At their closest approach, the objects positioned themselves directly in front of the cockpit, approximately 500 feet ahead. The interior of the 747 cockpit blazed with intense illumination, and Captain Terauchi reported feeling distinct warmth on his face — a thermal effect that suggested genuine energy emission. The lights shifted through colors described variously as orange, green, white, and amber. Co-pilot Tamefuji characterized them as "Christmas assorted" colors, pulsating slowly rather than strobing.

Terauchi attempted to photograph the objects but struggled with an unfamiliar camera. Flight Engineer Tsukuba also tried to capture images, but his autofocus failed in the darkness. No photographic evidence would be obtained.

The Mothership

After approximately 10 minutes, the two smaller objects departed as suddenly as they had arrived. What came next profoundly affected the crew. Against the twilight-darkened sky to the left of the aircraft, Captain Terauchi observed a massive, dark silhouette — a walnut or Saturn-shaped craft with a wide, flattened rim and pronounced bulges above and below its center. He estimated its size at "twice the size of an aircraft carrier" — roughly 1,600 to 2,000 feet across.

The large object exhibited no visible wings or tail. Its surface appeared to have structural features including what Terauchi described as "turrets" or circular indentations. Unlike the smaller, brightly illuminated craft, the mothership displayed only faint amber or pale white lights, possibly positioned on each end of its circumferential rim.

This massive craft maintained station with the 747 for approximately 30 minutes, pacing the aircraft through course changes, altitude adjustments, and evasive maneuvers. Terauchi reported that the object appeared to stay deliberately on the darker side of the sky, as if avoiding clear illumination — a strategic positioning that seemed to suggest intentional control.

Multi-Source Radar Confirmation

What distinguishes the JAL 1628 encounter from countless other aviation UFO reports is the convergence of radar evidence from three independent sources, each capturing anomalous signatures during the encounter.

Onboard Radar: Captain Terauchi activated the 747's digital weather radar, setting it to a 20-nautical-mile range with no depression angle. A large green, round object appeared at 7 to 8 nautical miles in the 10 o'clock direction — precisely where the crew visually observed the objects. Terauchi noted that the radar return was colored green, the weakest echo color typically reserved for weather targets, despite what should have been an extremely strong return from a craft of such reported size.

Anchorage Air Route Traffic Control Center (AARTCC): Ground-based radar intermittently tracked an unidentified target near JAL 1628. The controller handling the flight documented repeated primary radar observations of an object maintaining proximity to the aircraft despite evasive maneuvers including a 360-degree turn and an altitude descent of 4,000 feet. The controller's written report stated that he "several times had primary returns where JL1628 reported the traffic" and that "the traffic stayed with JL1628 through turns and descent."

Elmendorf Air Force Base Regional Operations Control Center (ROCC): Military radar briefly detected what was described as a "surge primary return" — an intermittent radar echo unaccompanied by a transponder signal — positioned approximately 8 nautical miles from the JAL aircraft. At one point, military controllers reported tracking a "flight of two," suggesting they detected both the JAL 747 and an unknown target simultaneously. However, these military radar contacts were fleeting and soon disappeared from their display.

Notably, Fairbanks airport radar detected nothing unusual, further complicating the radar picture.

Air Traffic Control Response

Throughout the 50-minute encounter, Anchorage Center treated the situation as a potential traffic conflict and coordinated multiple response measures. Controllers directed Captain Terauchi to execute evasive maneuvers, including a descent from 35,000 feet to 31,000 feet and a complete 360-degree turn near Fairbanks. The crew reported that the unidentified object maintained its relative position throughout these maneuvers — a behavior consistent with active tracking and station-keeping rather than coincidental proximity.

At 5:40 PM, Anchorage Center vectored a United Airlines passenger flight northbound from Anchorage to pass near JAL 1628 in an attempt to obtain independent visual confirmation. The center directed the two aircraft to pass within five miles of each other at different altitudes. When the United flight reported visual contact with the JAL 747 against the darkening sky, the pilots stated they observed no other traffic or unidentified objects. Similarly, a USAF C-130 military transport aircraft vectored to investigate reported seeing only the JAL 747.

By the time other aircraft arrived in the vicinity, the unidentified objects had either retreated or become invisible. The mothership disappeared from the crew's view approximately 40 nautical miles north of Talkeetna, as the 747 continued its descent toward Anchorage. Flight 1628 landed safely at 6:20 PM.

The Crew's Professional Assessment

Immediately upon landing, the three crew members were met by FAA investigators and debriefed on the evening of November 17, 1986. FAA Security Manager Jim Derry and Security Inspector Ronald E. Mickle conducted preliminary interviews while the encounter remained fresh in the crew's minds.

Significantly, the FAA investigators documented that the crew was "normal, professional, rational" with "no drug or alcohol involvement." Captain Terauchi's military background, extensive flight experience, and 29 years with Japan Air Lines lent considerable credibility to his account. First Officer Tamefuji and Flight Engineer Tsukuba, though providing less elaborate descriptions than the captain, independently corroborated the presence of multiple bright lights that maneuvered in coordinated formations.

When asked to account for the objects' unusual characteristics, the crew rejected conventional explanations. Terauchi noted that no known aircraft could achieve the maneuverability or acceleration patterns they witnessed. The objects displayed no exhaust trails, no sonic booms, no turbulent wake effects — yet maintained precise station-keeping with a jumbo jet cruising at over 600 miles per hour.

The FAA Investigation and Initial Acknowledgment

The FAA launched a formal investigation into the incident, preserving over 1,500 pages of documentation including audio recordings of air traffic control communications, radar data, crew interviews, and controller statements. This level of institutional documentation was extraordinary for a UFO report — most aviation incidents of this type received cursory treatment or none at all.

Thirty-eight years earlier the Air Force had given an airline crew's report the opposite kind of attention. The estimate written after the Chiles-Whitted encounter of 1948 concluded the object was interplanetary, and the document was ordered destroyed.

In late December 1986, word of the encounter leaked to the Japanese press when Captain Terauchi spoke to journalists from Kyodo News. By December 29, UPI reporter Jeff Berliner broke the story in the United States. The Washington Post ran the headline on January 2, 1987: "UFO Sighting Confirmed by FAA, Air Force Radar; Japanese Crew Tells of Encounter Over Alaska."

Initially, FAA public affairs officer Paul Steucke confirmed to reporters that the agency's radar had detected intermittent returns near the JAL aircraft. The Air Force acknowledged that their radar at Elmendorf had picked up something, though Captain Robert Morris of Alaska Air Command attributed it to "random clutter or weather interference."

The Skeptical Response and Planetary Misidentification Theory

The rapid media escalation prompted swift action from organized skepticism. On January 22, 1987 — before the FAA had completed its investigation — the Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal (CSICOP), represented by prominent UFO skeptic Philip J. Klass, issued a press release titled "UFO Mystery Solved." Klass proposed that Captain Terauchi had misidentified the planets Jupiter and Mars, which were exceptionally bright in that sector of the sky on the evening in question.

The theory held that Jupiter, at magnitude -2.6, would have appeared approximately 10 degrees above the horizon on the left side of the aircraft. Mars would have been slightly lower and approximately 20 degrees to the right of Jupiter. Klass noted that despite Jupiter's exceptional brightness and Mars's visibility, Captain Terauchi never once reported seeing either planet — only the unidentified objects.

However, the planetary hypothesis faced significant obstacles. Multiple crew members reported seeing numerous stars in the sky, indicating they could distinguish astronomical bodies from the phenomena they witnessed. The objects they described exhibited movement incompatible with stationary celestial bodies — they changed formation from vertical to horizontal alignment, accelerated instantaneously, and maintained precise coordination with the 747's maneuvers. Additionally, the crew reported "too many lights" and "clusters of lights shaped like windows," descriptions bearing no resemblance to planetary appearance.

The Radar Artifact Explanation

On March 5, 1987, the FAA released its final investigation package at a press conference in Anchorage. The agency's conclusion attributed the radar anomalies to a "split radar image" — a technical artifact where an aircraft's primary radar return (skin paint) becomes separated on the radar display from its secondary return (transponder echo) due to timing misalignment.

FAA Technical Center specialist Dennis R. Simantel analyzed the radar data and concluded that the mysterious secondary target was likely an "uncorrelated primary and beacon target," a well-known radar phenomenon. The agency noted that such artifacts occurred frequently in Alaska's challenging radar environment due to the "critical timing associated with delay adjustments in the aircraft transponder and the target correlation circuitry within the radar equipment."

However, the FAA's technical explanation faced challenges. One of the AARTCC controllers later stated that they "rarely, if ever" experienced split image effects in the area where JAL 1628 was operating. Additionally, the unexplained radar blips were intermittent rather than consistent — appearing and disappearing rather than remaining constant as a true split image would. A genuine radar artifact should persist with each radar sweep as long as the aircraft remained in the radar's coverage area. This same tension — trained radar operators insisting an artifact explanation doesn't match what they actually saw on their scopes — recurs directly in the modern GIMBAL and GOFAST analysis, where AARO's parallax model similarly clashes with pilot testimony about the broader radar picture.

Critically, the FAA's March release neither confirmed nor refuted the visual sightings. The agency stated only that it "lacked the resources and congressional mandate" to investigate UFO sightings and had not attempted scientific analysis of astronomical or atmospheric explanations. The official position became: the radar evidence is inconclusive, the visual evidence is uncorroborated, therefore the incident remains unresolved.

The Professional Consequence

In December 1986, after speaking to Japanese journalists, Captain Kenju Terauchi became an international news figure. Japan Air Lines — embarrassed by the publicity and concerned about corporate image — responded decisively. Terauchi was removed from flight status and reassigned to a desk job, effectively grounding one of their most experienced pilots.

This action sent a clear signal through the aviation community: pilots who report UFOs in public forums face career jeopardy, regardless of their experience level or the credibility of their accounts. Terauchi was eventually reinstated to flight duties after several years and later retired from JAL, never recanting his account but speaking of it with resignation about institutional skepticism.

The professional consequences faced by Captain Terauchi contributed to a broader pattern of pilot reluctance to file UFO reports. Many experienced aviators, fearing ridicule or career damage, chose silence over transparency — the same stigma pattern that Navy pilot Ryan Graves would describe decades later, testifying that only around 5 percent of UAP sightings ever get reported.

Government Briefing and Alleged Suppression

Years after the incident, FAA Division Chief John Callahan revealed previously undisclosed details about an internal government response. In his public testimony, Callahan stated that he had compiled the JAL 1628 evidence — including synchronized video of the radar data and voice tapes — and presented it to FAA Administrator Donald Engen in early January 1987.

Engen arranged a classified briefing attended by representatives of the CIA, FBI, and President Reagan's scientific study team. According to Callahan's account, at the conclusion of the presentation, a CIA official declared the meeting classified and stated that the event "never happened." Callahan claimed that all presented materials were confiscated. The pattern — credible officials compiling real evidence, only for an intelligence official to declare the incident officially non-existent — is structurally identical to the "unacknowledged special access program" dynamic later described in the Immaculate Constellation whistleblower testimony, nearly four decades apart.

However, Callahan retained copies of the original files in his office, which he later provided to UFO researchers. Thanks to his foresight, the core documentation survived and was eventually made public through Freedom of Information Act requests. In 2001, researcher John Greenewald of The Black Vault filed FOIA requests seeking the JAL 1628 materials. After the FAA initially claimed the records had been destroyed, Greenewald persisted for 17 years until he located over 1,500 pages of documentation in the National Archives in 2018.

Competing Hypotheses

The JAL 1628 case presents an unusual challenge because the evidence simultaneously supports and resists conventional explanation. Multiple hypothesis frameworks have emerged.

The Anomalous Phenomenon Hypothesis: Proponents argue that the combination of detailed, consistent crew testimony, onboard radar confirmation, military radar detections, and multi-source air traffic control documentation suggests a genuine unknown aerial object with non-conventional properties. The crew's descriptions of extreme maneuverability, silent operation, and apparent intelligence in avoiding visual confirmation by other aircraft are inconsistent with known 1986 aircraft technology. Independent researchers including optical physicist Bruce Maccabee and Dr. Richard Haines (former NASA researcher and NARCAP founder) have argued that the evidence, taken holistically, points to an unexplained phenomenon rather than misperception or instrument error.

The Radar Artifact and Misidentification Hypothesis: Skeptics argue that the visual observations can be attributed to Jupiter and Mars combined with atmospheric optical effects (ice crystal reflections, light refractions), while the radar anomalies represent standard technical artifacts. Philip Klass pointed to inconsistencies in crew testimony and Terauchi's history as a "UFO repeater" (having reported two prior sightings and two subsequent ones in January 1987). When the FAA later explained that the January 11, 1987 sighting involved lights from small villages diffused by thin ice-crystal clouds, skeptics cited this as validation of their approach.

The Inconclusive Evidence Assessment: Most neutral observers acknowledge that the case presents genuine anomalies in both radar and visual domains that defy easy resolution, yet also recognize that the evidence falls short of proof. The lack of photographic evidence, the failure of vectored aircraft to obtain independent visual confirmation, and the intermittent nature of radar detections leave the case in a state of genuine ambiguity.

Document Preservation and Historical Recovery

The FAA initially compiled an extensive investigation package released on March 5, 1987, including over 150 pages of radar printouts, ATC transcripts (approximately 23 pages), crew interviews, controller statements, flight path charts, and simulated radar imagery. This represented an unusually thorough documentation of an aviation UFO incident — a standard that remains unmatched in most subsequent cases.

However, in 2001 and again in 2009, when John Greenewald filed FOIA requests for the materials, the FAA responded that the records had been destroyed in accordance with federal records retention schedules. This development prompted Greenewald to pursue the materials through the National Archives directly. After 17 years of effort, he located the complete case file at the National Archives in Seattle, recorded under Record Group 237, containing over 1,500 pages of documentation.

This recovery proved historically significant. The rediscovered materials confirmed the contemporaneous accuracy of the 1987 public release and demonstrated that government agencies had indeed preserved the incident's documentary record, despite initial claims of destruction. The materials are now publicly accessible through The Black Vault, ensuring the historical record remains available for independent analysis.

Unresolved Questions

The JAL 1628 incident, despite decades of investigation and public debate, leaves several critical questions unanswered:

Object Identity: What were the three objects observed by the JAL crew? No conventional explanation fully accounts for the described characteristics — the extreme maneuverability, silent operation, ability to maintain precise station with a high-speed aircraft, apparent intentional behavior, and the scale of the largest object.

Radar Discrepancy: Why did onboard radar clearly detect the objects while ground-based radar confirmation remained intermittent and inconsistent? This pattern suggests either peculiar radar cross-section characteristics or a phenomenon with properties unlike conventional aircraft.

Third-Party Confirmation Failure: Why did the United Airlines flight and USAF C-130 fail to obtain visual confirmation despite vectoring to the reported location? Possible explanations include the objects' retreat upon approach, their destruction of visual distinctiveness against the darkening sky, or deliberate avoidance of observation.

Physical Effects: What caused the reported cockpit illumination and facial warmth? These physical effects suggest genuine energy emission rather than misperception, yet no instrumental verification of thermal or electromagnetic radiation was obtained.

Forty Years On

November 17, 2026 will mark the 40th anniversary of the encounter. In that time, the institutional landscape around UAP reporting has changed dramatically — AARO now exists specifically to investigate these cases, and four PURSUE tranches have declassified hundreds of similar military encounters. Yet JAL 1628 still stands largely alone in its combination of triangulated radar sources, cockpit-level physical effects, and over a thousand pages of contemporaneous government paperwork. Few of the cases declassified in 2026 approach its evidential density, which says as much about how rarely this kind of documentation survives as it does about the case itself.

1,500 Pages of Documentation, No Explanation

On November 17, 1986, Captain Kenju Terauchi and his crew experienced a phenomenon that remains fundamentally unexplained by official government channels. Despite over 1,500 pages of FAA documentation, radar data, voice recordings, crew testimony, and decades of analysis by independent researchers, the nature and origin of the objects they encountered have never been conclusively determined.

The case endures not because it proves extraterrestrial visitation or definitively identifies unknown aircraft, but because it demonstrates the limitations of conventional investigation frameworks when confronted with genuinely anomalous phenomena. The encounter challenges fundamental assumptions about what is and isn't possible in controlled airspace and raises persistent questions about the adequacy of current detection systems, investigation protocols, and institutional openness regarding unexplained events.

As aviation safety and national security communities continue to grapple with unidentified aerial phenomena, the JAL 1628 incident serves as a historical touchstone — a reminder that even extensively documented, multi-source confirmed encounters can resist conclusive explanation. The case invites continued examination not to prove any particular hypothesis, but to understand the boundaries of our knowledge and the importance of rigorous, transparent investigation of the truly unknown.
