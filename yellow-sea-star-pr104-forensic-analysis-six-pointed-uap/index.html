The Yellow Sea "Star": What PURSUE's Six-Pointed UAP Video Actually Shows

PURSUE Release 4 included infrared footage of a six-pointed "star" over the Yellow Sea. We verified the file's chain of custody and ran it through frame-by-frame forensic analysis, including a negative control and an optical simulation.
Share
ChemaVX

When the Pentagon's fourth PURSUE release included infrared footage of an object over the Yellow Sea resembling a six-pointed star, we noted it in our coverage of that release as a geometry unlike anything in the previous three tranches. It deserved a closer look. So we got the actual file, verified its chain of custody, and ran it through the same kind of frame-by-frame forensic analysis we applied to the viral Roswell footage last week.

Getting the Real File

The clip in question is designated DOW-UAP-PR104, credited to AARO and reported by U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, hosted on DVIDS — the Pentagon's official public media distribution service. We pulled two independent copies: the official 1920×1080 high-resolution master, and a separate remux of the public HLS stream that requires no account. Both hashed identically in structure — same resolution, same 30fps, same 470 frames, same 15.667-second duration — and every measurement in this analysis was replicated across both copies within 2 degrees, ruling out the possibility that what follows is an artifact of one particular encoding.

The official DVIDS record lists the location as the Yellow Sea, categorizes the clip as B-roll, and dates it "01.01.2025" — a date that, given the description only commits to "in 2025," is almost certainly a placeholder rather than the actual incident date, a pattern repeated across other PURSUE video entries. One genuine discrepancy stood out: the official description states the report consisted of "18 seconds of video footage," but the published file — verified in both copies — runs exactly 15.67 seconds. Checked against five other PURSUE videos from two different release tranches, every one of them matches its stated duration exactly. PR104 is the only outlier. Roughly 2.3 seconds of reported footage did not make it into the public file, and there's no way to determine from public material whether that reflects a redaction during declassification or a drafting error on the official record.

What the Footage Actually Shows

The frame contains a compact, extremely high-contrast core surrounded by six thin radial spikes, arranged in three collinear pairs passing directly through the core — one vertical pair, two diagonal pairs forming an X. The spikes extend more than 300 pixels, roughly a sixth of the frame width, tapering progressively outward. At least eight black censorship boxes cover the positions where sensor telemetry would normally appear — coordinates, heading, field of view, platform identification — confirming the clip was deliberately edited before release. An unredacted cyan tracking overlay remains visible: targeting brackets, a north indicator, and a contrast-tracking gate consistent with a gyrostabilized electro-optical/infrared turret actively following the target against a gray ocean background.

The Test That Settles It

The decisive question is simple: does the six-pointed shape belong to the object, or to the sensor looking at it? A physical object with six radiating arms would need to maintain an attitude locked to the optical axis of an independently gyrostabilized camera for the full 15 seconds — an implausible coincidence. An optical artifact, by contrast, is generated inside the sensor's own aperture, so it stays locked to the screen by construction, regardless of how the camera pans.

The footage was tested directly. Averaging sixteen frame crops centered on the core sharpens the six spikes into crisp, well-defined lines rather than blurring them — mathematically impossible if the spike orientation had varied relative to the frame between shots. Meanwhile, the ocean background visibly shifts between frames as the sensor pans to track its target. A separate, more granular test measured the rotation of the spike pattern across 63 frames sampled four times per second: the average apparent rotation came out to 0.55 degrees, with no progressive drift over time — statistically indistinguishable from zero. A platform panning and drifting in flight for fifteen seconds would produce measurable, progressive rotation in any structure physically attached to the object it's filming. None was found. The star is locked to the sensor, not to whatever is generating the heat signature underneath it.

A radial brightness profile confirms the same story from a different angle: contrast falls off smoothly and continuously from the core outward — the signature of a point-spread function with glare, not the sharp-edged silhouette of a solid object.

A Negative Control From the Same Release

To confirm the method wasn't simply predisposed to find artifacts everywhere, the identical analysis pipeline was run against PR46 — a separate AARO/Indo-Pacific Command video from the first PURSUE tranche, showing an officially described "football-shaped body." PR46 uses the exact same HUD design as PR104: identical corner-bracket geometry, the same north indicator in the same relative position, the same censorship pattern — strong evidence both clips came from the same sensor family and the same declassification pipeline. But PR46's object behaves completely differently under analysis: an elongated bright body whose structure and orientation move together with the object between frames, not with the screen, and without the long, thin radial spikes seen in PR104.

This is exactly the discriminator the instrumental-artifact hypothesis predicts. The same sensor system, looking at an actual extended object, does not produce a screen-locked star. Looking at an intense, unresolved point source, it does.

An Optical Simulation Reproduces It Exactly

The diffraction-spike hypothesis was tested a third way: by building a physical optical model. A point source viewed through a circular aperture with three linear obstructions, oriented to match the observed spike angles, run through a standard diffraction point-spread-function calculation across nine wavelengths, with detector saturation and blooming added to match the footage's black-hot presentation. The simulation reproduced all six spikes in three collinear pairs at angles matching the real footage to within half a degree, along with the saturated core and the continuous radial falloff. This isn't just a plausible label attached after the fact — a three-parameter physical model of the sensor's own optics generates the exact pattern seen in the clip.

This is also a well-documented phenomenon well beyond this one video. Six-point diffraction stars appear in any optical system with three internal obstructions or a hexagonal aperture — it's the same physics that gives the James Webb Space Telescope its six characteristic diffraction spikes from its segmented mirror supports. Independent analysts on the forensics forum Metabunk identified the same pattern in this clip within days of the PURSUE Release 4 publication, and noted an almost identical case from May 2026 — a Ukrainian drone video showing an identical six-pointed infrared star, resolved the same way: the spikes appeared and disappeared with camera movement while the star itself stayed level with the screen.

What Remains Genuinely Unknown

None of this identifies what's actually generating the heat signature. The censored telemetry removes exactly the fields that would allow it: platform, field of view, sensor mode, coordinates. What's verifiable is that the turret is a gyrostabilized IR system with automatic contrast tracking, operating from an Indo-Pacific Command platform over the Yellow Sea — consistent with several sensor families used on maritime patrol aircraft in that theater, though the censorship makes a specific identification impossible from public material alone. The underlying thermal source — flare, engine plume, aircraft seen against a thermal background — cannot be determined from what's public. Nor can the real date of the incident, the content of the missing ~2.3 seconds, or the object's actual distance, size, or speed, since no range data survived the redaction.

This distinction matters and is worth stating precisely: AARO's "Unresolved" label means the case lacks a formal analytical conclusion — largely because the government's own redactions removed the data that would allow one — not that a conventional explanation has been ruled out. The star shape itself is not the mystery. It's an artifact of the sensor's own optics, reproducible with a three-parameter model, confirmed against a negative control from the same release, and consistent with a known phenomenon documented independently in an unrelated case months earlier.

Why This Kind of Verification Matters

PURSUE has now released four tranches and hundreds of files across eight decades, examined across our coverage since May. Most of that material — Apollo transcripts, Cold War records, congressional testimony — can only be evaluated through the credibility of witnesses and the internal consistency of official statements. Video is different: it can be measured. The same instinct that led us to run frame-by-frame forensics on the viral Roswell tape applies here — a six-pointed "star" is a striking, shareable image, and striking, shareable images are exactly the kind of claim that deserves to be tested against the file itself rather than against the caption someone wrote for it.

That doesn't make the case boring. It makes it precise. Something intensely hot triggered a gyrostabilized military tracking system over the Yellow Sea in 2025, closely enough that AARO logged it and the Pentagon redacted the surrounding telemetry before release. What that heat source actually was remains, honestly, unresolved. What it was not is a six-armed object.
