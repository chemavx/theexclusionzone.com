The USS Nimitz 2004 Tic Tac UAP Encounter: Declassified Military Evidence of Extraordinary Aerial Phenomena

Four naval aviators, multi-platform radar, and declassified infrared video. The 2004 USS Nimitz Tic Tac encounter remains the most thoroughly documented military UAP case in the public record.
Share
ChemaVX

On November 14, 2004, off the coast of Southern California, the USS Nimitz Carrier Strike Group encountered unidentified aerial phenomena that demonstrated flight characteristics contradicting known physics. A retired Navy commander, trained weapons systems officers, and radar specialists witnessed events that remain officially unexplained decades later. This encounter represents one of the most credible and well-documented military UAP incidents in American history.

The November 2004 Events: What Happened

In early November 2004, the USS Princeton's advanced AN/SPY-1B Aegis radar system detected unusual aerial contacts appearing at altitudes exceeding 80,000 feet, then descending rapidly toward sea level while maintaining speeds of approximately 100 knots. These objects exhibited no identification friend-or-foe (IFF) signals and operated outside established flight corridors. The radar tracked them for nearly a week before November 14, when the Nimitz Carrier Strike Group decided to launch an intercept.

Commander David Fravor of Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (the Black Aces) piloted one F/A-18F Super Hornet, with Lieutenant Commander Jim Slaight as his weapons systems officer. Lieutenant Commander Alex Dietrich flew the second aircraft with her WSO. Vectored by the USS Princeton toward a contact at approximately 20,000 feet and 60 miles distant, the pilots expected a routine intercept.

Upon arrival at their designated coordinates, all four aircrews—Fravor, Dietrich, and their respective WSOs—observed a large disturbance in the ocean below. The water churned noticeably despite calm sea conditions with no whitecaps from wind. Hovering approximately 50 feet above this white-water disturbance was a white, featureless object roughly 40 feet in length, shaped like a capsule or "Tic Tac" breath mint. The object exhibited no visible control surfaces, rotors, or exhaust plumes.

As Fravor descended in a clockwise circle to investigate, the object mirrored his maneuvers, ascending to meet him at an equivalent rate. The object then shifted its orientation, aligning longitudinally with Fravor's aircraft. When Fravor attempted to cut across the circle to achieve a nose-on intercept at approximately half a mile distance, the object accelerated rapidly and vanished from sight. Fravor later stated it "accelerated like nothing I've ever seen," disappearing in less than a second—faster than an aircraft traveling at Mach 3 would disappear under normal circumstances.

The USS Princeton's radar controller then reported that the object had reappeared at the fighters' pre-planned Combat Air Patrol (CAP) point, a location roughly 60 miles away, in less than one minute. This translocation suggested either instantaneous acceleration exceeding 45,000 mph or a coordinated repositioning indicating intelligent control.

The Second Intercept and FLIR Video Evidence

Approximately one hour later, a second sortie launched from the USS Nimitz. This flight included an F/A-18F equipped with an AN/ASQ-228 Advanced Targeting Forward-Looking Infrared (ATFLIR) pod. Lieutenant Commander Chad Underwood, serving as the weapons systems officer, detected a radar contact approximately 33 miles to the south. Underwood locked the targeting pod onto the contact and recorded the now-famous FLIR1 video.

The infrared footage shows a bright, featureless thermal signature against the cold sky background. As Underwood's aircraft attempts to maintain targeting lock, the object executes lateral and longitudinal accelerations, with the gimbal-mounted camera struggling to track the rapid movements. The video captures several seconds of erratic motion, including what appears to be the object accelerating to the left, causing the automatic tracking system to lose lock momentarily before reacquiring the target.

Underwood coined the term "Tic Tac" to describe the object's shape, and emphasized during later interviews that the object's behavior violated normal aerodynamic principles. He noted that conventional aircraft must obey laws of physics governing lift, drag, and propulsion—none of which appeared to constrain this object.

Multi-Sensor Corroboration

The credibility of the Nimitz encounter derives from corroboration across independent sensor systems and trained observers: The same question returns with each release: when the Pentagon's fourth PURSUE batch produced infrared footage of a six-pointed object over the Yellow Sea, the geometry matched nothing in the earlier record.

Visual Confirmation: Four experienced naval aviators—two pilots and two weapons systems officers—provided consistent accounts of an object with specific visual characteristics. Their descriptions remained stable across interviews conducted years apart.

Radar Tracking: The USS Princeton's AN/SPY-1B radar system, one of the most sophisticated combat systems in existence, tracked the object(s) over multiple days. The radar operated at the threshold of detection capabilities for a small object, yet provided consistent range, altitude, and heading data. Senior Chief Operations Specialist Kevin Day supervised the radar operations and later testified that the system had been recalibrated to eliminate false returns, yet continued detecting the contacts.

Infrared Video: The ATFLIR pod recorded thermal imagery that the Department of Defense officially confirmed as authentic and unmodified in 2019. Unlike visual or radar data, infrared signatures provide a semi-independent confirmation mechanism.

Absence of Conventional Explanations: No aircraft in the U.S. Navy inventory, no foreign known platform, and no known natural phenomenon matched the observed characteristics—particularly the combination of hovering capability, controlled maneuvering at extreme g-forces, lack of visible propulsion, absence of thermal signature inconsistent with jet engines, and acceleration profiles that should produce sonic booms under normal aerodynamic conditions.

Pilot Testimony and Analysis

Commander Fravor provided detailed sworn testimony to the House Oversight Committee in July 2023. He emphasized that the object he encountered was fundamentally unlike any aircraft he had observed during decades of military aviation experience. The object exhibited instantaneous directional changes, lacked visible control surfaces or propulsion systems, produced no rotor wash despite hovering, and accelerated beyond any known aerospace capability.

When pressed on whether the object could have been a classified U.S. system, Fravor responded that, as one of the top 20 highest-ranking members of the carrier group at the time, he would have been briefed on any military test operations in the area. No such briefing occurred.

Lieutenant Commander Underwood stated that the object's behavior as captured on his ATFLIR "wasn't behaving within the normal laws of physics." He emphasized that while pilots can misinterpret visual phenomena, the ATFLIR's objective sensor data—which tracks objects using thermal imaging—should provide reliable target characterization independent of observer bias.

What AARO Has Officially Concluded About the Nimitz Tic Tac

For all the attention the encounter has drawn, one question still has no clean answer: what has the U.S. government's own investigative body formally determined about it? The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), established in 2022 as the Pentagon's permanent UAP investigation arm, has never published a formal case resolution declaring what the Tic Tac was. As of 2026, the incident remains officially categorized as "unidentified" — and the public record contains two government-linked assessments that flatly contradict each other. AARO's predecessor closed its files in 1969: Project Blue Book logged 12,618 sightings and left 701 unexplained.

The first comes from Dr. Sean Kirkpatrick, AARO's founding director. Near the end of his tenure, Kirkpatrick characterized the Tic Tac as an unresolved case largely because, by his account, no raw sensor data from the 2004 encounter was preserved in a form AARO could re-analyze. Without that data, he argued, a definitive conclusion was impossible. He also floated possible conventional explanations — among them that the object could have been a CIA balloon or a classified project under test. Critically, these were framed as possibilities, not findings: AARO under Kirkpatrick reached no formal determination resolving the case.

The second assessment cuts directly against that framing. An 18-page analysis authored by Jay Stratton — a veteran intelligence official who led the Pentagon's UAP Task Force — examined the encounter in detail and referred to the object as an "Anomalous Aerial Vehicle" (AAV). Stratton's report reaches six conclusions, the most consequential being that the AAV was of unknown origin and represented technology not in the possession of the United States or any other nation. Stratton stated he had personally ruled out the possibility that the Tic Tac was a secret American platform. The report also notes that the USS Louisville, a nuclear attack submarine operating with the strike group, detected no unusual acoustic signatures during the event — a detail that complicates simpler explanations rather than resolving them.

The gap between these two positions is the real state of the official record. One government-linked assessment treats the case as an open unknown attributable to missing data; another concludes the object was advanced technology belonging to no known nation. AARO has issued no public report adjudicating between them. The Department of Defense's position remains unchanged from its 2020 statement: the phenomenon is "unidentified," a designation that confirms only that identification has not been achieved — not that an explanation has been found and withheld, and not that anomalous origin has been established.

For anyone searching for AARO's "official conclusion" on the Nimitz Tic Tac, then, the honest answer is that no such conclusion exists. What exists instead is an acknowledged unknown, a former director's unconfirmed speculation, and a separate government analysis asserting the object's origin lies beyond any nation's known capability — with the underlying sensor data that might settle the question still unreleased, and according to some accounts, never preserved at all.

Official Government Response and Declassification

For over a decade, the Pentagon maintained silence on the incident. In December 2017, The New York Times published investigative articles revealing the existence of the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), a Pentagon initiative that had studied the Nimitz encounter and other UAP cases since 2007. The articles included the FLIR1 video released by the Department of Defense.

In September 2019, the U.S. Navy officially confirmed that the released videos depicted genuine unidentified aerial phenomena recorded by naval aviators. On April 27, 2020, the Department of Defense formally released three Navy UAP videos—FLIR1, GIMBAL, and GOFAST—alongside an official statement confirming that the phenomena depicted remained "unidentified."

Technical Analysis of Flight Characteristics

Physicist Kevin Knuth and researchers from the Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies (SCU) conducted kinematic analysis of the 2004 incident, estimating lower-bound accelerations from pilot testimony and radar data. Their 2023 peer-reviewed paper, "Estimating Flight Characteristics of Anomalous Unidentified Aerial Vehicles," concluded that the object demonstrated accelerations ranging from approximately 5,400 to 46,500 g's—vastly exceeding human tolerance, structural limits of known aerospace materials, and sustainable acceleration profiles for any known propulsion system.

The paper noted that acceleration from 28,000 feet to sea level in 0.78 seconds, as reported by radar operator Kevin Day, would require approximately 13,000 gigawatts of power—exceeding the total nuclear power output of the United States. Energy release at this scale would produce equivalent force to approximately 200 Tomahawk cruise missiles detonating simultaneously, yet no such effects were observed.

Controversy Over Data Retention

Several enlisted witnesses aboard the USS Princeton and USS Nimitz later claimed that unidentified officials appeared aboard the ships shortly after the November 14 incident and confiscated or directed the destruction of electronic data recordings. Petty Officer Gary Voorhis reported that individuals arrived by helicopter, wore generic flight suits, and collected AEGIS system data tapes. Aviation Technician Patrick Hughes described being ordered to surrender hard drives from E-2C Hawkeye radar records.

Commander Fravor publicly disputed these accounts, stating that no "men in black" appeared on the ships and that any tape removal was likely the result of normal rotation and reuse of recording media. However, Fravor acknowledged that beyond his own limited observations, he could not speak to all shipboard activities. The Navy has not released a comprehensive data package from November 2004 via Freedom of Information Act requests, leaving the extent of original data loss unresolved.

Data survives when somebody outside the chain of command keeps a copy. The FAA file on the 1986 JAL 1628 encounter over Alaska exists today because an official kept his own set of the interviews and the voice tapes after the agency closed the case.

Congressional and Intelligence Community Recognition

The incident gained official recognition when Congress mandated that the Director of National Intelligence compile a preliminary assessment of UAP. Released in June 2021, that report examined 144 UAP incidents reported between 2004 and 2021, with only one conclusively identified (a weather balloon). The report stated that 18 incidents demonstrated unusual flight characteristics including "high velocity, turn rates, and apparent lack of identifiable means of propulsion."

The 2004 Nimitz encounter is recognized as one of the most significant cases in that assessment. U.S. Senators, particularly former Intelligence Committee Chairman Marco Rubio, explicitly referenced the Nimitz incident during congressional deliberations, noting that if the observed capabilities represented foreign adversary technology, the strategic implications would be severe.

Scientific and Defense Community Context

The incident prompted the establishment of the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force in August 2020, subsequently reorganized as the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) in 2022. These organizations represent the first permanent, officially-mandated government structures focused on investigating UAP as a potential national security concern.

Physical scientists have noted that the Nimitz encounter's characteristics align with theoretical requirements for interstellar travel. The estimated acceleration profiles—if sustained—would permit reaching relativistic velocities sufficient to traverse interstellar distances within human-scale timeframes. However, no scientist has asserted that this confirms an extraterrestrial origin; rather, it indicates that if the observed data are accurate, the object's capabilities exceed conventional aerospace engineering by orders of magnitude.

Unresolved Questions and Classification Status

Two decades after the encounter, fundamental questions remain unanswered:

  • What is the precise nature of the object's propulsion system and how does it generate the observed accelerations without conventional thrust vectors or observable exhaust?
  • How did the object achieve directional changes that would produce g-forces lethal to biological pilots without evidence of pilot protection systems?
  • What accounts for the lack of electromagnetic radiation or thermal signature consistent with known propulsion methods?
  • Why was the incident not formally investigated at the time, and why have subsequent official investigations not been publicly disclosed?
  • Why does the Department of Defense continue to classify analysis of the incident and related data?

The U.S. Navy maintains that the phenomenon remains "unidentified." This designation reflects the military's position that current available data do not support identification of the object's origin, nature, or operational purpose.

Legacy and Ongoing Impact

The Nimitz encounter has served as a catalyst for policy change within the U.S. military. The Navy updated pilot reporting procedures in 2019 to encourage unrestricted reporting of unusual aerial observations without stigma. Pilots who subsequently reported similar encounters—including those aboard the USS Theodore Roosevelt in 2014-2015—cited the Nimitz precedent as providing institutional credibility to their reports.

The incident has also influenced scientific inquiry, with researchers at institutions including Harvard University (Galileo Project) and NASA's Planetary Science Institute incorporating UAP data into formal research protocols. The combination of credible witnesses, multi-sensor confirmation, and official government documentation creates an evidentiary foundation that scholars and policy analysts argue merits rigorous scientific investigation.

The Best-Documented Military Encounter on Record

The 2004 USS Nimitz Tic Tac encounter represents the most thoroughly documented military UAP incident in the public record, combining trained observer testimony, multi-platform sensor data, official video confirmation, and published kinematic analysis. The phenomenon observed contradicts current understanding of aerospace capabilities and physics, yet remains officially unexplained after two decades. Regardless of ultimate resolution, the incident has permanently shifted military, congressional, and scientific attention toward systematic investigation of unidentified aerial phenomena as a legitimate domain of inquiry rather than a dismissed cultural curiosity.
