USS Russell 2019: The Pyramid UAP Video and the Channel Islands Drone Swarm

In July 2019, U.S. Navy destroyers near California's Channel Islands were swarmed by unidentified objects. Leaked night-vision footage sparked "flying pyramid" theories — the Navy says drones, but the operators were never identified.
Share
ChemaVX

On a summer night in July 2019, multiple U.S. Navy warships operating near California's Channel Islands encountered something that defied immediate explanation. Objects appeared on radar, hovered above decks, and pursued vessels across the Pacific. Months later, leaked night-vision footage would spark global headlines when the triangular shapes resembled "flying pyramids"—a characterization later explained by optical science, but one that crystallized the mystery surrounding an incident the military still hasn't fully resolved.

The Multi-Night Swarm: July 14–30, 2019

First Encounters Near San Clemente Island

The first documented sighting occurred on the night of July 14, 2019, when sailors aboard the USS Kidd, an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer, spotted two unidentified drones hovering near the ship around 10 p.m. Within minutes, the USS Rafael Peralta confirmed additional objects. The vessels immediately entered "River City," a naval communications lockdown designed to minimize electronic emissions. By 11:23 p.m., the USS Rafael Peralta logged something remarkable: a white light hovering motionless over its flight deck while the destroyer maintained a forward speed of 16 knots and visibility was less than one nautical mile—conditions that commercial drones simply cannot sustain.

The encounter lasted over 90 minutes, far exceeding the endurance of any commercially available unmanned aerial system. A passing cruise ship, the Carnival Imagination, radioed the USS Rafael Peralta to report they too had observed between five and six unidentified objects in the vicinity, confirming they were not from the civilian vessel.

The Russell Video and Night-Vision Mystery

The most famous documentation came on July 15, 2019, when the USS Russell's SNOOPIE team (Ship Nautical Or Otherwise Photographic Interpretation and Exploitation)—specialized sailors trained in photographic intelligence—captured footage through night-vision equipment. The brief video showed triangular-shaped lights hovering above the destroyer at approximately 700 feet elevation. The objects appeared to hover, flash in red, green, and white sequences, and move in ways inconsistent with typical aircraft behavior.

A SNOOPIE team leader narrated one encounter: "We have visual of four probable unidentified drones, with course unknown and speed unknown. Contacts are operating at a range of a thousand yards, and holding. Tail numbers are unknown."

Extended Operations Through July 30

The incidents were not isolated events confined to two nights. Pentagon documents released under Freedom of Information Act requests reveal that multiple Navy vessels—including the USS Russell, USS Kidd, USS Paul Hamilton, USS Bunker Hill, USS Ralph Johnson, and USS Omaha—experienced drone activity across multiple dates in July. On July 17, the USS Paul Hamilton made an abrupt right turn after drones flew past the vessel. Radar aboard the USS Bunker Hill tracked objects reaching altitudes of 21,000 feet. The USS Ralph Johnson logged as many as 10 additional visual sightings across a period spanning several hours.

The phenomena continued into July 30, when the USS Russell experienced another encounter with five unknown drones, prompting deployment of SNOOPIE teams in the early morning hours and activation of the ship's DRAKE system—a portable radio-frequency jammer designed to disrupt drone control signals.

The Pyramid Shape: Optical Illusion or Mystery?

When filmmaker Jeremy Corbell released the USS Russell footage in April 2021, the internet erupted. The triangular appearance of the objects sparked immediate speculation: Were these "flying pyramids"—evidence of otherworldly technology? Defense officials were forced to explain what had captivated millions.

Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence Scott Bray provided the answer during a May 2022 congressional hearing. "The triangular appearance is a result of light passing through the night vision goggles, and then being recorded by an SLR camera." This explanation aligns with optical physics: night-vision scopes possess apertures—the opening that allows light through—and some have triangular or hexagonal shapes. When photographed through these apertures using a camera lens, out-of-focus light sources take on the aperture's geometric shape, a photographic effect known as bokeh.

Skeptical investigator Mick West had reached this same conclusion independently within days of the footage's release. He noted that other lights visible in the same video also appeared triangular, which would be inexplicable if the objects themselves were pyramid-shaped, but perfectly consistent with a camera aperture effect. The "flying pyramids" were likely conventional drones or aircraft, their appearance distorted by the very equipment used to observe them in darkness.

The Investigation: Drones, But From Where?

The Navy's response was immediate and classified. Within days, investigative teams from Naval Criminal Investigative Service, the FBI's Los Angeles field office, and the Coast Guard began examining the incidents. Chief of Naval Operations Admiral Michael Gilday was briefed on July 18. The investigation ultimately classified, cutting off further public disclosure.

Pentagon officials later confirmed that Navy investigators determined the objects were unmanned aerial systems—drones—based on comparison with a separate similar incident that occurred off the East Coast. However, the operators' identities remained unknown. This distinction is critical: the Navy identified the objects as drones, but could not identify who deployed them or why.

Suspect Vessels and False Leads

Investigators initially focused on the Hong Kong-flagged bulk carrier MV Bass Strait, which was in the vicinity during some incidents. Navy briefing slides assessed that the Bass Strait was "likely using UAVs to conduct surveillance on US Naval Forces." However, subsequent analysis revealed the ship was docked in Long Beach—approximately 100 miles away—during portions of the swarm activity, making it an unlikely source.

The ORV Alguita, a catamaran in the area, was also investigated. The vessel did carry drones aboard, but they were DJI Phantom IV quadcopters with maximum flight times of 28 minutes and ranges of only a few miles—far insufficient to match the observed capabilities. Investigators ruled out Navy operations after checking with Fleet Area Control and Surveillance Facility San Diego, which showed no scheduled UAV flights during the encounter periods.

Inexplicable Capabilities

Whatever the objects were, their demonstrated performance exceeded known commercial drone technology. Based on Navy deck logs and ship positions reconstructed through Automatic Identification System data, The War Zone calculated that objects traversed at least 100 nautical miles during operations. They maintained station-keeping over moving vessels traveling at 16 knots in low-visibility conditions. Radar aboard the USS Bunker Hill tracked contacts climbing to 21,000 feet altitude. Multiple objects coordinated simultaneously across swarms of four to eleven units.

Defense contractor experts consulted by journalists noted that maintaining position at 21,000 feet for extended periods while illuminated (as some objects were described) would require energy capacity far beyond standard quadcopters, whose batteries last approximately one hour under ideal conditions. Operating at high altitude with visual strobes increases power drain exponentially, yet objects remained aloft for hours.

Official Conclusions and Lingering Questions

In May 2022, Deputy Director Bray presented the incident as a "solved" case during a congressional hearing on unidentified aerial phenomena—one of the few UAP incidents the government claims to have definitively identified. Bray stated the Navy was "reasonably confident" the objects were drones, based on correlation with the later East Coast incident.

Yet fundamental questions persist. Who operated the drones? From where were they launched? What was their mission? Why were they flown so openly and persistently in restricted military training airspace near San Clemente Island, a Navy facility hosting sensitive operations including SEAL training and live-fire exercises? Why did multiple defensive measures—including five rounds fired by the USS Russell on July 20—fail to definitively resolve the situation?

Chief of Naval Operations Gilday, when asked about the incidents in 2021, stated the Navy could not identify the drones but ruled out extraterrestrial origin. Official statements have since characterized the incidents as solved, though the operators remain unidentified—a peculiar resolution that addresses the objects' nature but not the security breach itself.

The Broader Context: Trans-Medium Claims and Competing Narratives

While Navy officials classified the Russell incident as a drone swarm, other 2019 incidents released through FOIA created additional intrigue. The USS Omaha captured infrared footage of a spherical object descending into the ocean without apparent destruction. A submarine search for wreckage proved unsuccessful. Filmmaker Corbell and Navy sources he interviewed described this as evidence of "trans-medium" technology—craft capable of operating in both air and water.

Corbell claims he has communicated with dozens of sailors, including one aboard the USS Russell, who reported witnessing craft "accelerate instantaneously into the upper atmosphere." He asserts that crews logged objects as "drones" due to rapid assessment protocols and limited categorization options available at the moment, but that witnesses questioned whether the label was accurate.

A Navy active-service member involved in incident reporting told Corbell that the "drone" designation "in hindsight was inaccurate," reflecting that the observed craft "was not positively identified as such." Yet official Navy briefing slides persist in categorizing the objects as "Quadcopter style UAS."

Counter-Measures and Military Response

The Navy's defensive actions escalated throughout July. On July 20, the USS Russell fired five rounds at the drones in a counter-unmanned-aerial-system exercise, with at least one documented misfire. Ships deployed SNOOPIE teams multiple times across multiple nights. By July 23 and July 30, vessels activated DRAKE systems—portable radio-frequency jammers manufactured by Northrop Grumman designed to interrupt drone control signals.

The USS Russell also deployed SCAT teams (Small Craft Action Teams), typically tasked with defending against small-boat attacks, indicating the Navy treated the incursion as a potential security threat warranting the most severe on-ship response protocols.

Broader Implications: Drone Swarms as Emerging Threat

The 2019 incidents occurred amid a documented global surge in drone swarm incidents. Between 2019 and 2022, the cybersecurity firm DroneSec registered approximately 151 swarm incidents worldwide, with marked increases in activity. Criminal organizations and nation-states recognized the value of coordinated multiple-drone operations for overwhelming security systems, conducting surveillance, or staging diversionary attacks.

A 2020 Department of Justice audit documented a notable incident in which 15 drones were deployed simultaneously to distract and overwhelm a prison facility's defenses. U.S. military leadership identified drone swarms as "the most concerning tactical development since the rise of the improvised explosive device in Iraq," according to Marine General Kenneth McKenzie Jr.

China and Russia have invested heavily in drone swarm capabilities specifically designed for maritime environments, viewing them as potentially decisive in future conflicts over Taiwan or other contested regions.

The Classified Investigation and Information Gaps

Despite the incidents occurring in restricted military training areas with sophisticated sensor platforms aboard advanced destroyers, the investigation was classified shortly after briefing the Chief of Naval Operations. Email records released through FOIA show investigators were still seeking information on July 25, 2019—well after the initial incidents—when emails reference a "classified briefing on drones," with subsequent details denied due to classification.

The Navy possesses among the most advanced sensors on earth aboard these Arleigh Burke-class destroyers: AN/SPY-1B radar, AEGIS combat systems, Forward-Looking Infrared systems, electronic surveillance equipment, and sonar arrays capable of detecting minute underwater disturbances. Yet these collective capabilities apparently proved insufficient to definitively identify the drones' operators or conclusively determine their characteristics beyond the basic determination that they were unmanned.

An Unexplained Security Breach

Whether the 2019 Channel Islands drone swarms represented foreign adversary surveillance, a covert domestic test, or something else entirely, the Navy has not publicly divulged. What is certain is that unidentified aerial objects operated with apparent impunity near the most sensitive U.S. naval training facilities for weeks, despite investigation involving multiple federal agencies and briefing the highest levels of Navy command.

The Pentagon's characterization of the incident as "solved" because the objects were identified as drones—while the operators, origin, mission, and purpose remain unknown—suggests the resolution prioritizes classification over closure. As tensions escalate with China over emerging drone swarm technologies and defense officials warn of persistent vulnerabilities to unmanned threats, the unanswered questions from July 2019 loom larger than ever.

Future disclosure of classified investigation files may finally answer who was watching the watchers off California's coast.
