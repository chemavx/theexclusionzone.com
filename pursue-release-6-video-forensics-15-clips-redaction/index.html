PURSUE Release 6 Video Forensics: Four Results, Three of Them Negative

We downloaded the fifteen videos of the Pentagon's sixth PURSUE tranche and measured them. Every stated duration checks out, the PR104 diffraction star does not recur, nine of ten military clips share a redaction template, and the 1952 Tremonton film is the only one with nothing covered up.

ChemaVX

When the Department of War published its sixth PURSUE tranche on 18 September 2026, we said in our coverage of that release that we would download the fifteen videos and measure them the same way we measured the sixteen from the fifth, and publish whatever came out — whether or not it matched what had been reported. This is that.

Four results. Three of them are negative, and the negative ones are the reason to trust the fourth.

Getting the Files at All

war.gov returns HTTP 403 to us and has for weeks. The route that works runs through the Internet Archive: the Wayback Machine holds a snapshot of war.gov/UFO/release/06/?type=.vid taken on 21 September, and inside that page is the file that actually matters — uap-data.csv, the catalogue the department's own portal reads to build its interface. It carried 447 rows the day we pulled it — today it carries 450, and the difference is Colorado — one per item across all six tranches, with a column of DVIDS video identifiers. DVIDS serves the files without complaint.

All fifteen downloaded: 673 MB. Every measurement below was taken from those files, not from a description of them.

Result One: the Stated Durations Are Correct

Twelve of the fifteen clips state a running time in their catalogue entry. All twelve match the file to within one second — the gap is rounding to whole seconds and nothing else.

That matters because of what we found in the fourth tranche's PR104, where the official description claimed 18 seconds and the published file ran 15.67. Roughly 2.3 seconds of reported footage never reached the public file. We have now checked twelve more clips across a third tranche. PR104 remains the only outlier in the series. Whatever happened there did not happen again.

Result Two: the Six-Pointed Star Does Not Repeat

Six of the fifteen come from U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, five of them over the Yellow Sea — the same body of water and the same sensor family as PR104, whose six-pointed "star" we showed was a diffraction artefact generated inside the camera's own aperture, reproducible with a three-parameter optical model.

The obvious hypothesis was that the pattern would recur. It does not. We pulled frames from all six and none of them shows the radial spike structure: a compact hot return, a contrast-tracking reticle, a north indicator, and nothing else. PR144 and PR152 are shot against cloud rather than sea; PR150 is almost entirely dark.

That is worth stating plainly because it constrains the earlier finding rather than extending it. The PR104 artefact belonged to one optical configuration, not to a theatre and not to a sensor family.

Result Three: What the Redaction Actually Covers

This is the one positive finding, and it took four attempts to get right.

Nine of the ten military electro-optical clips carry a near-identical redaction footprint. Measured as the share of the active frame permanently covered by solid black boxes:

  • DOW-UAP-PR141 — 9.6%
  • DOW-UAP-PR140 — 9.5%
  • DOW-UAP-PR144 — 9.8%
  • DOW-UAP-PR148 — 9.8%
  • DOW-UAP-PR143 — 10.0%
  • DOW-UAP-PR151 — 10.3%
  • DOW-UAP-PR133 — 10.3%
  • DOW-UAP-PR152 — 11.5%
  • DOW-UAP-PR135 — 12.7%
  • DOW-UAP-PR150 — 22.1% (the exception)

Median 10.3%, and nine of the ten fall inside a three-point band. The boxes sit over the positions where sensor telemetry is rendered — coordinates, heading, field of view, platform identification — which is consistent with a standard template applied at declassification rather than case-by-case judgement about what each clip reveals.

PR150 is the exception at more than a fifth of the frame, and it is also the smallest file in the tranche at 908×512.

And the One Clip With No Redaction At All

DOW-UAP-PR159 — the Project Blue Book film of reported UFOs over Tremonton, Utah, on 2 July 1952 — carries no redaction. Zero. The 25% of its frame that reads as black is pillarboxing: a 4:3 film placed inside a 16:9 container.

Two independent sources agree on this. Our measurement finds no fixed black regions inside the active image, and in the department's own catalogue PR159 is the single row of the fifteen whose Redaction field is blank. Everything else in the tranche is flagged TRUE.

A seventy-four-year-old film needs nothing hidden. The 2022-2025 material does.

What We Could Not Measure

The four Colorado police videos defeat this method and we are not going to publish a number for them. They are vertical phone footage shot at night, where most of the frame is genuinely dark: the permanently-black share reads between 26% and 43%, but the per-frame share runs from 55% to 92%, and that gap is scene, not redaction. Separating a black box from a black sky needs a different approach than the one used here.

There is a second reason, and we did not see it until we went back to the catalogue. On the Colorado clips the redaction is not in the picture: it is in the sound. AARO stripped the original audio "to protect information whose release could reasonably be expected to affect the privacy or other protected interests of a U.S. person or commercial entity", and published transcripts instead — themselves redacted, carrying the (b)(7)(A) and (b)(7)(C) exemptions over the reporting officer's identity. Those transcripts were not in the catalogue we counted in our tranche-six piece: they appeared afterwards.

How This Nearly Went Wrong Four Times

The redaction figure above is the fourth number we produced. The first three were wrong, each for a different reason, and we are publishing the failures because they are the only evidence that the fourth is sound.

First, a regular expression read the stated durations as wildly short. It captured only the seconds of "four minutes fifty-eight seconds" and reported PR133 as running 240 seconds longer than declared. A five-fold discrepancy across half the tranche would have been a dramatic finding. It was a parsing bug, visible only because the errors were suspiciously round.

Second, pillarboxing was counted as censorship. PR159 came out at exactly 25.0% — which is precisely what side bars occupy when 4:3 content sits in a 16:9 frame. The number that looked like a finding was arithmetic about aspect ratios.

Third, moving boxes read as zero. The method required a pixel to be black in every sampled frame, which is the right test for a fixed overlay and the wrong one for a box that tracks something. PR133 returned 0.0% while its frames visibly carry black rectangles.

Fourth, the threshold was off by two. PR133's boxes are drawn at greyscale value 8, not 0. A cutoff at 6 excluded 92,584 pixels — 10.05% of the frame — and would have let us report that one clip in the tranche was released unredacted. The department's own catalogue said otherwise, and the catalogue was right.

Each of those four would have produced a publishable-looking claim. What caught them was not care; it was having something to check against — an aspect ratio that explained a number too cleanly, a frame we had actually looked at, a metadata field that disagreed. A measurement with nothing to contradict it is not a measurement.

What Remains Unknown

None of this says what the objects are. The redaction removes exactly the fields that would allow range, size and speed to be derived, and it removes them consistently across nine of ten clips, which means the question is closed by construction rather than by analysis. What is now established is narrow and verifiable: the files are the length they claim to be, the PR104 diffraction artefact was specific to that clip, the military redaction follows a template of roughly a tenth of the frame, and the only material in the tranche that nobody felt the need to cover up is from 1952.

Update, 17 October 2026: the four Colorado clips, measured

When this piece went out we wrote that the four Colorado police videos defeated the method, that we would not publish a number for them, and that separating a black box from a black sky needed a different approach than the one used here. We have written that approach. Here is what it says.

The short answer: there is not a single redaction box in any of the four.

Why counting black pixels could not work

In those clips the permanently-black share runs between 26% and 43%, and the per-frame share reaches 92%. They are vertical phone recordings — 608×1080 — shot at night: most of the frame is dark because it is the sky, not because anything was placed over it. Any measure built on how much black there is counts the night as redaction.

What marks a box is not its colour but its shape. A redaction fills its own bounding rectangle almost completely, has straight edges, and does not move for the length of the clip. A night sky covers a large region with a ragged boundary — the horizon, the treeline — and leaves its bounding box half empty.

The first filter, the one for fill, was not enough, and we found that out by running it over the clips themselves: two of the four Colorado files reported a "box" covering 33.6% and 11.1% of the frame. Neither has any redaction at all. The reason is geometric. A band of sky runs edge to edge and starts at row zero, so its bounding rectangle is well filled even when its lower boundary is a horizon. The filter that does separate them is edge straightness: column by column you check where the black region begins and ends, and in a box those profiles are constant while across a horizon they wander.

We are writing this down for the same reason we published the four wrong numbers further up: a filter that fails on real footage is the only evidence that the one we kept does not.

The validation, which is the part that matters

A method that finds nothing is worth nothing until it has been shown to find something. So before pointing it at Colorado we ran it over material where the redaction is confirmed: three of the ten military clips in this same tranche, the Yellow Sea files, pulled from DVIDS like the rest.

clip boxes share of frame
DOW-UAP-PR143 7 10.0%
DOW-UAP-PR144 5 9.0%
DOW-UAP-PR150 6 22.1%

The first two land around the 10.3% median published above, and this time they come out of a program written from scratch, without consulting the earlier one. The boxes sit where we said they sat: in the corners and along the edges, over the positions where the sensor renders its telemetry. The largest in PR143 is 238×279 pixels in the bottom-right corner; another, 182×157, occupies the top-left. All seven fill 100% of their bounding rectangle.

With the same program, and without touching a single threshold, the four Colorado clips return zero.

What looked like redaction and is not

In one of them the lower half of the frame is visibly smeared, and that is the kind of thing a glance files as censorship. It is not. Measured row by row, the luminance climbs from 10 to 96 across roughly a hundred pixels, steadily, with no step in the texture. That is a ramp, and it is what an illuminated foreground outside the focal plane produces — precisely what you get filming at night from a vehicle. A post-production mask leaves a step at one specific row, because it is a rectangle. In the military files that step is there — in PR143 the texture falls fifteenfold from one band to the next — and in the Colorado files it appears in none of the frames examined.

The audio: the stated claim holds

The documents say AARO stripped the original audio. That is the department's assertion, and until now we had quoted it without checking it. All four files carry a sound track, so the easy reading would have been that it was never removed.

Measured, the track is empty: −91.0 dB mean and −91.0 dB peak across all four. The peak is the figure that settles it, because a single audible instant would still leave a low mean. This is not the volume turned down. It is digital silence from start to finish.

The stated durations check out

Each transcript states how long the video it transcribes runs. Nobody had held that against the file.

transcript states the file runs difference
LLE-UAP-D002 15 minutes 900.167 s +0.167 s
LLE-UAP-D003 27 seconds 27.840 s +0.840 s
LLE-UAP-D004 1 minute, 42 seconds 102.467 s +0.467 s

All three agree within the rounding the documents themselves use. No footage is missing — unlike the Yellow Sea clip from the previous tranche, where 2.3 seconds were.

The fourth, LLE-UAP-D001, cannot be checked either, and for a reason we did not expect: it states no duration at all. It took a day to recover — it was not in the release we downloaded and the Internet Archive was offline — and when we opened it, it turned out not to be like the other three. Its matching video, PR001, runs 79.967 seconds, and there is nothing in the document to hold that figure against.

The fourth transcript does not match the other three

LLE-UAP-D001 came from an Internet Archive capture dated 23 September 2026. Its PDF carries no text layer — it is an image — so we read it with OCR at 300 dpi, and the lines quoted below were confirmed under three different recogniser settings before we wrote them down.

It departs from its three siblings in two ways. It does not say how long the video it transcribes runs, where the other three say so in their opening sentence. And it does not mention AARO stripping the audio, where the other three state it in the same fixed wording; this one speaks only of editing the text to protect identities.

There is a third difference, and it is the one we cannot explain. 48% of the words in LLE-UAP-D001 fall inside runs of five or more words that also appear in LLE-UAP-D004, the transcript dated three months later. Two of those runs are not administrative boilerplate:

"okay the craft is flying closer to me now I can see it" — fourteen consecutive words, identical in both documents.

"I did check radar there is no aircraft flying in the area" — twelve consecutive words, identical in both documents.

They are not the same video. PR001 runs 79.967 seconds and PR004 runs 102.467. Measured over the same sampled frames, the mean luminance of one is 15.8 and of the other 2.4: the second is six times darker. The near-black share of the frame is 68.3% and 91.7%. These are two different recordings.

Several explanations fit and we have no data to choose between them. It may be the same officer describing two similar events in the same words — the January document speaks of "another orb", which is consistent with an earlier one. It may be a transcription error, with text carried over from one document to the other. And it may be one event recorded as two clips with one of them misdated, which we cannot rule out because the catalogue already dates PR004 to October 2023 against its own title.

What is verifiable is the overlap itself, and that it comes out of the department's own two documents rather than anyone's reading of them.

One detail from the catalogue

The LLE-UAP-PR004 row in uap-data.csv gives the incident date as October 2023. Its own title says January 2024, its transcript says January 2024, and the officer recording it says in the first second that it is 2 January 2024. This is a metadata error, not a revelation; we note it because it is not the first time this release's catalogue has disagreed with itself.

What this does not establish

No boxes does not mean the material is intact. The redaction in these four clips is where we already said it was — in the stripped audio and in the transcripts themselves, carrying the (b)(7)(A) and (b)(7)(C) exemptions — and none of that is visible in the picture. Nor can we rule out a cut: we measure what is there, not what is missing, and a clean excision leaves no trace in the duration if the document was written afterwards.

The shape measurements are taken over twelve frames spread across each clip. A box that appeared and vanished within a short stretch could slip past that sampling, though it is not how the military files in this tranche are redacted, where the boxes are present throughout.

The program that does all of this is published with its controls, so anyone can repeat it over the same files and get the same figures. It is what we ask of anyone making a claim about a video.
