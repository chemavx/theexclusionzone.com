Fragments 1962-1964: The Word They Deleted from NASA's Public Role

Two files from the seventh PURSUE release, 73 pages dated 1962-1964, set out who collects, analyzes and speaks about falling space debris. A 1963 memo records that DOD and the Air Force wanted NASA as 'cover', and the public draft deletes the word 'analyzing'.
Share
ChemaVX

Somewhere in the seventh PURSUE release there is a draft policy document with its own editing key printed at the top: brackets mean material deleted, underlining means material added. In the first paragraph, the word sitting inside the brackets is "analyzing".

The document is called "Guidelines for Interagency Handling of Space Vehicle Fragments", and it is one of two files — seventy-three pages between them, dated 1962 to 1964 — that set out what the United States government did when an object came down out of the sky and somebody had to establish where it came from.

They are not about UFOs. That is worth saying at the top, because of where they have been published. They are about rocket and satellite debris, American and Soviet, falling on other countries' territory at the height of the space race. What makes them worth reading is that they are the procedure itself: who collects the object, who takes it apart, who decides what happens to it, and who speaks in public.

The division of labour

Paragraph one designates NASA as the agency responsible for receiving and identifying fragments, or suspected fragments, of space vehicles of all types — "including the contents thereof" — which fall on United States territory or otherwise come into its possession. The exception is carved out immediately: the Department of Defense is solely responsible for fragments from vehicles the Department of Defense launched.

The rest distributes everything else with the same flat precision. DOD will tell NASA when deorbits are expected and when collection teams have been alerted, and will report the results of those collection efforts whether successful or not. The Atomic Energy Commission joins the analysis of any fragment that carried — or is suspected of having carried — radioactive or special nuclear material. The State Department provides policy guidance on what finally happens to fragments from foreign-launched vehicles.

And NASA receives and identifies. The analysis, in the public version, is simply gone: the same deletion appears in the draft public statement, which reads "responsible for receiving [analyzing], and identifying [any] fragments".

The one-page memo that explains it

A separate file, dated 29 May 1963, is a single page addressed to Dr. Welsh — E. C. Welsh was executive secretary of the National Aeronautics and Space Council, a body inside the Executive Office of the President — and signed by R. W. Hale. Subject: "Conference with Frutkin — Fragments". Arnold Frutkin ran NASA's international programmes.

Hale lists seven things Frutkin thinks. No public announcement is needed. An Executive Order or a White House statement "would scare people needlessly". The problem is overseas, and State would like to be able to point at NASA as the responsible US agency "without a scary public announcement".

Then point four, which is the one that explains the brackets: "DOD and AF want to use NASA for a 'cover' but have no intention of letting NASA do anything but return some pieces." The quotation marks around cover are in the original.

Point five notes that the CIA and NASA get along fine and can continue to do so on this matter. Point six, that NASA should wait and see what the United States Intelligence Board settles on. Point seven closes the circle: although NASA had been drafting legislation, they would oppose sending anything to Congress in the foreseeable future "because of the harmful debate it would touch off".

How high this went

On 15 November 1963, the Space Council circulated the draft to seven men, each with his agency written beside his name: the AEC, the CIA, DOD, NASA, the National Security Council, the Office of Science and Technology and the State Department.

Three weeks earlier it had gone higher. On 24 October 1963 the same office wrote about "Responsibility and Procedure re Space Vehicle Fragments" to Secretary of State Dean Rusk, Director of Central Intelligence John A. McCone, Deputy Defense Secretary Roswell L. Gilpatric, NASA Administrator James E. Webb, the director of the Office of Science and Technology, and McGeorge Bundy, the president's special assistant for national security.

Agreement was not universal. A December 1962 memo refers to "the small hassel between DOD and CIA" over the procedural guidelines, judges that no White House intervention is desirable or needed, and then concedes there is "no guarantee that this could not become a White House issue on strictly jurisdictional grounds". Its author calls the whole thing a tempest in a teacup.

What to say if anyone asks

The other half of the file is the communications plan, and it is written with care. In May 1964, with debris from the SA-6 booster in orbit, NASA circulated the agreed response to any questions about fragments hitting the ground. It went to Wernher von Braun at Marshall, among others, and it opens: "No queries have been received. No information is being volunteered." It notes the text was coordinated with the State Department, and that any answer beyond the script may come only from the Administrator, Deputy Administrator or Associate Administrator.

Two of the prepared lines matter. One: "Under no circumstances does this agency (government) conjecture on the origin of an object. Rather, it desires to obtain, analyze, and attempt to establish the origin of such an object." The other, for once a fragment is known to be on its way in: the object will be studied and "prompt public announcement will be made when analysis has been completed".

The pencil

An internal question-and-answer sheet from April 1964 runs through the awkward ones. Where would fragments land — mostly ocean, dispersed over thousands of square miles. Would blowing up the orbiting element help — no, it would make more fragments.

Question 7 asks whether being hit by a fragment would be lethal, and the answer carries something a transcript cannot show: more than half of it is scratched out by hand, in pencil. What survives is the bare part — in the remote possibility of being struck, it might, depending on the area of body struck, be lethal. What was struck out is the reassurance that followed: that the same is true of a meteorite, that being hit by a meteorite is likelier than being hit by a fragment from "(satellite X)", and a figure from the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory for how many meteorites over 2.2 lbs reach the earth each year.

That parenthesis gives the document away. It is a template, with a slot for whichever satellite was falling that month. And somebody with a pencil decided the meteorite argument would not be used.

What is still closed

The file opens with an LBJ Library document withdrawal sheet — the inventory of what was taken out of the folder before release. The folder is "Space Vehicle Fragments — Responsibilities and Procedures", box 018, in the national security files of Charles E. Johnson.

It lists a dozen-odd items pulled: drafts of the guidelines, Space Council meeting records, a "Welsh to McCone" dated 31 May 1963. The restriction code on nearly all of them is the same — closed under the executive order governing access to national security information. Some carry a later opening date pencilled in. Others do not.

What this is, and what it isn't

It is not evidence of anything recovered that nobody can explain, and nothing in these seventy-three pages hints otherwise. It is the baseline: the written answer to who handles an object of undetermined origin that falls from the sky, agreed between the civilian space agency, two military departments, the CIA, the AEC and the State Department, and signed off at the level of the president's national security adviser.

That answer was: put a civilian agency in front. Record internally that the military wanted that civilian agency as cover. Take the word "analyzing" out of the public description of what it does. And volunteer nothing.

Anyone arguing about recovered materiel should know this document exists, not because it proves their case, but because it is what the real thing looked like — with names, dates, and the deleted word still sitting visibly inside its brackets.
