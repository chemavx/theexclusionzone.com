Utah Ranch 2010: What the AAWSAP Contractor Measured, and What Its Report Stops Short of Claiming

On 23 October 2026 the Department of War released DOW-UAP-D174, a BAASS field report from two 2010 deployments to a Utah ranch. The method is sound, the subject is not, and the report claims to have found nothing.

ChemaVX

The AAWSAP contract has been argued about for eight years on the strength of interviews, leaks and recollection. On 23 October 2026 the Department of War published sixty pages of the thing itself: a field report, on the contractor's own letterhead, describing exactly what two deployments to a ranch in Utah were spent on. It is a strange document, because the method is sound and the subject is not, and because it does not claim to have found anything.

The file is DOW-UAP-D174, catalogued as "Utah Ranch Investigation Report, April 2010" and dated by the catalogue to March and April of that year. The author is BAASS — Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies — the company that executed the Defense Intelligence Agency's AAWSAP contract between 2008 and 2012. It is partially redacted: witness and investigator names are withheld under the codes 1843(a)(2) and 1843(a), the redaction scheme this declassification programme uses.

One thing the document does not do is name the property. It is "the ___ Ranch" throughout, in Fort Duchesne, northeastern Utah. Readers will draw their own conclusion about which ranch that is; the file does not make it for them. What the file does contain is the word "Skinwalker" — not as the investigators' framing, but inside the accounts of the people they interviewed.

Two deployments, and what they installed

The report covers 22-26 March and 19-23 April 2010. Four BAASS personnel travelled to Fort Duchesne: two investigators and two scientists, one identified in the text as a biologist. The stated purpose was to "locate evidence of the reported unidentified creatures and lights, gather additional detailed descriptions, locate and interview new witnesses", and to build out a camera network.

They installed seven Moultrie digital trail cameras across the ranch and a neighbouring property, with a protocol for BAASS security personnel stationed at the ranch to collect the memory cards weekly.

The project abstract lists four aims. Two are what you would expect of an intelligence contract: interview the surrounding population comprehensively "in order to delineate the scope and variety of eyewitness reports", and use portable instruments to measure baseline magnetic field, EM spectrum, ionising radiation and soil chemistry.

The other two are why this document is worth reading. The third is to use plants, "including transgenic plants", as biosensors for radiation and magnetic-field deviations. The fourth, verbatim: "To initiate a variety of games, puzzles, and other strategies in order to establish communication with the source/origin of reported anomalies on the ranch property."

The accounts

As of 7 May 2010 the report says more than twenty-five people had been contacted and interviewed, eleven of them describing first-hand encounters. Ages ran from fifteen to seventy-seven; the events described ranged from twenty years earlier to April 2010.

The findings are set out in five numbered points. Eleven separately interviewed witnesses described a large "dog" or "wolf" like creature able to walk and run on two legs, six to seven feet tall when upright — and the report allows that the interviews may describe "at least three separate types" of creature. Four independent witnesses described small, hairy, three-foot figures with long arms that moved "in a similar way to chimpanzees or orangutan". More than two witnesses reported large "worm" like creatures, a "pterodactyl" like bird, "little people" and other anomalies, with the report noting that the great majority of these accounts fall within the preceding five to seven years. Interspersed with all of it were sightings of unidentified aircraft and "balls of light".

The fifth point is the only one that produced anything physical. BAASS obtained photographs of tracks in snow that "remain unidentified": in one set two separate trails merge into one, and in another the tracks appear to walk "through" a barbed wire fence. Hair samples were collected near those trails and were, at the time of writing, undergoing DNA analysis.

The executive summary adds an incident from the night before the second deployment arrived. A family being re-interviewed said that at around 2300 they heard a loud howl from immediately outside the south side of the house — the same sound they associate with the creature. They described a "growling roar", twice, five to ten seconds each time, apparently produced by a large animal that was standing still, and ruled out a coyote or a person.

The experiments, which are real experiments

On 6 April 2010 the team brought six spiderwort and six tobacco plants to the ranch, three of each in two different rooms, under grow lights cycling sixteen hours on and eight off. The tobacco assay is not improvised. It uses a naturally occurring mutation at the Sulfur gene: heterozygous Su/+ plants are pale green, and exposure to a mutagenic agent shows up as visible spots — dark green where one allele is knocked out, white or yellow where the other is. This is a mutagenesis bioassay used in actual laboratories.

On 22 April they set up a germination experiment in three places at once: two rooms of the ranch trailer and a control at BAASS's own facility. Sugar snap peas and green beans, forty-nine seeds of each type per tray, in reusable elongated pots under timed grow-light systems, watered with distilled water at every location. The point was to compare germination and growth rates across the three sites.

That is a competent design: it has a control, it fixes its environmental variables, and it asks a falsifiable question. What it does not have yet is a result, and the report says so plainly — at the time of the April deployment the plants had been at the ranch for only two weeks, so "no observations or analysis can be drawn" about mutation levels in either species.

The signal, and what the report says about it

The instrumental finding that will travel furthest from this document is also the one it explains most carefully. During electromagnetic signature sweeps of the property, one location on the mesa recorded two strong signals, in both horizontal and vertical polarisation, that had not been present in the previous day's readings at a different point. The first was at 1210, 1213 and 1216 MHz; the second at 1232.5, 1235.5 and 1238.5 MHz.

The report then does the thing that matters. It notes that those frequencies fall in the 1164-1215 and 1215-1240 MHz bands, spectrum allocated to Earth exploration-satellite, radiolocation, radionavigation-satellite and space research, and that the provisions governing them reserve the space primarily for military use while allowing other federal agencies to use it for science and research. It closes the paragraph by conceding that "further measurements and research, particularly into satellite transmitting frequencies and times will be required to identify the origin of these signals".

Put plainly: a contractor measured unexplained signals sitting in the bands where satellite navigation lives, wrote that down, and recorded that it had not ruled out the obvious. Nowhere does the document assert that the signals were anomalous.

What is left open

Three things, and the report leaves them open itself. The DNA analysis of the hair samples was in progress and no result appears here. The track photographs remain unidentified. And both botanical experiments had barely started.

What is now on the record, with dates, equipment models and a catalogue number, is what a slice of the DIA's AAWSAP money actually bought: ten days in the field across two deployments, seven trail cameras, more than twenty-five interviews, twelve biosensor plants, three trays of ninety-eight seeds each, and a spectrum sweep. For a programme whose shape has been reconstructed largely from testimony, that is a different kind of source.

It should not be stretched past what it holds. This report does not establish that anything its witnesses describe exists, and it does not try to; it is a status update on an ongoing investigation, written for the client paying for it. Its documentary value is in the method and in what the method reveals about the programme's priorities — not in its conclusions, because it reaches none.

The seventh release contains twenty-seven other documents, including nine more from the same contract — 2009 monthly reports, a project management plan, and the file labelled Project Capella — and a 2026 memorandum on a triangular object in the western United States that deserves its own piece. From earlier releases we have already closed out the count for Release 6 and the forensic analysis of its fifteen videos.
