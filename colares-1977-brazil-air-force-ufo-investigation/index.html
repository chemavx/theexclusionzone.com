Colares 1977: Brazil's Most Documented UFO Investigation

In 1977, Brazil's Colares Island experienced over 300 documented UFO sightings and 120+ medical cases of alleged beam injuries. The Brazilian Air Force's Operation Prato captured photographs and film, yet the case remains officially unresolved.

In the second half of 1977, residents of Colares—a small fishing island in Brazil's Amazon delta—began reporting something that went beyond strange lights in the sky. They described narrow beams descending from luminous objects at night, striking people and leaving small burns and puncture-like marks, followed by weakness and dizziness. The reports grew alarming enough that the town's mayor asked the military for help, and the Brazilian Air Force launched an official field investigation, Operação Prato ("Operation Saucer"). It remains one of the most document-rich and genuinely strange UFO episodes in Latin America—and also one of the most contested.

An Island on Edge

Colares sits in the estuary northwest of Belém, in the state of Pará, a community of a few thousand people who lived by fishing and small-scale farming. In the 1970s it was reachable mainly by river, relatively isolated from the cities. Through August and September 1977, reports of strange aerial lights had been spreading through the region; locals gave the phenomenon a vivid name, "Chupa-Chupa" ("suck-suck"), from rumors that the beams could draw blood. By October, residents described nightly visitations: silent objects hovering over rooftops and riverbanks, and beams that seemed to seek people out.

The community's response was one of real fear. Villagers kept night watches, lit bonfires, and set off fireworks to drive the lights away. Fishermen grew reluctant to go out on the water. When the mayor appealed to Brasília for protection, what arrived was not an armed guard but an investigation team.

Operação Prato: A Military Investigation

Belém's regional Air Force command formed a field unit under Captain Uyrangê Hollanda and sent it to Colares in October 1977. Over roughly four months, the team interviewed witnesses across Colares and dozens of surrounding villages, collected accounts, and attempted to photograph and film the phenomena, equipped with cameras and optical instruments. That a national air force mounted a sustained, on-the-ground effort to document reported UFO activity is what sets Colares apart from most cases, which are reconstructed after the fact by civilian researchers.

By the accounts that survive, the operation gathered a large body of material—witness testimony from hundreds of people, hundreds of photographs, and many hours of Super-8 film. Hollanda himself reported seeing luminous objects at close range and described their movements as deliberate, even methodical. The investigation wound down in early 1978.

The Problem of the Evidence

Here the case becomes more complicated than dramatic retellings suggest. Much of what is known about Operação Prato's findings rests on a narrow base. Hollanda gave only a couple of interviews about the case, near the end of his life in the 1990s, and a great deal of the detail—the photo and film counts, the character of what was captured—traces back to his testimony and to copies he had kept, alongside witness and victim reports from local newspapers. By his account, the Air Force retained the bulk of the film and never returned it.

Official declassification came slowly and partially. Brazilian researchers petitioned for release in the mid-2000s, and the Air Force transferred a batch of documents to the National Archives around 2008–2009. Those releases corroborated some of the previously circulating material, but key photographic and film holdings remained restricted or unaccounted for. The result is a case that is simultaneously famous for its documentation and hampered by the fact that the most striking parts of that documentation have never been fully, independently examined.

The Injuries—and the Skeptical Reading

The most extraordinary element is the reported physical harm. The local physician on Colares, Dr. Wellaide Cecim Carvalho, examined residents who came to the clinic during the flap and described a recurring pattern: small burns and puncture-like marks, sometimes with reddening and a feeling of heat, accompanied by weakness, dizziness, and lingering fatigue. Her testimony is central to the case's reputation as one in which a UFO event allegedly caused bodily injury.

It is important to be clear about what this evidence is and isn't. Dr. Carvalho's observations are a single clinician's contemporaneous accounts, not a verified, independently reviewed medical archive; no confirmed radiation readings, recovered debris, or laboratory analyses corroborate the more dramatic characterizations that later circulated. Skeptics offer a coherent alternative: in a frightened, isolated community swept by nightly rumors of blood-sucking lights, panic and suggestion can amplify ordinary ailments and minor marks into a shared narrative of attack—a mass-psychogenic dimension that the conditions at Colares would have favored. Proponents counter that the consistency of the marks, and the fact that uniformed investigators themselves reported seeing the lights, pushes the case beyond simple hysteria. Both readings remain live; neither has been closed off by hard physical evidence.

What the Air Force Concluded

Operação Prato did not end with a confident public verdict. The operation was formally wound down in early 1978, and the surviving documentation reflects caution rather than identification—logs describing low-altitude structured lights and unexplained phenomena, without firm attribution to extraterrestrial craft, secret military hardware, or natural causes. That very hesitancy is part of why the case endures: a professional team spent four months on it and came away without a tidy explanation.

The episode also had a lasting institutional effect. Brazil went on to develop more formal channels for logging UFO/UAP reports from military and civilian pilots, and the country's relatively open posture toward releasing such material—however incomplete in the Colares case—stands in contrast to the secrecy of some other governments. Like Brazil's later Varginha incident of 1996, Colares became a touchstone in Latin American UFO lore, drawing researchers, documentaries, and curious visitors to the region for decades afterward.

An Air Force Investigation That Never Reached a Verdict

Colares is a genuinely unusual case: a sustained, on-the-record military investigation into reported aerial phenomena that allegedly harmed civilians, conducted not by enthusiasts but by an air force, and never resolved into a confident official answer. What it is not—at least not yet—is the airtight body of physical proof it is sometimes presented as. The most extraordinary claims rest on testimony from a small number of sources, much of the visual record remains restricted or lost, and a serious skeptical account rooted in fear and suggestion sits alongside the anomalous one.

That tension is the real heart of Colares. Something happened on that island in 1977 that frightened a community, mobilized a military investigation, and unsettled the officers who took part. Whether it was an encounter with something genuinely unexplained, a wave of panic given shape by rumor, or some mixture of both, cannot be settled until the full archive—the photographs and the hours of film—is finally opened to independent eyes. Until then, Colares remains documented, disputed, and unresolved: a mystery whose most important evidence is still, decades later, partly out of view.
