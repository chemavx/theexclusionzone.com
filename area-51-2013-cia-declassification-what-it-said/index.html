Area 51 in 2013: What the CIA Actually Admitted, and What It Didn't

In June 2013 a FOIA request forced the CIA to release a document that finally named Area 51. It wasn't a UFO disclosure — it was the administrative history of the U-2 and OXCART spy plane programmes, and it explains why the base generated so many 1950s UFO sightings in the first place.

For more than fifty years the United States government declined to admit the place existed. It appeared on no official map, in no public budget line, and every question about it drew the same administrative non-answer. Then, in June 2013, a Freedom of Information Act request forced the CIA to release a document that named it, put it on a map, and explained what it was built for.

The answer was less exotic than the legend. It was also considerably more interesting than the legend's defenders tend to admit — and it has been misreported ever since, including by outlets covering the 2026 disclosure wave.

The document, and what it actually is

The text that changed the situation is not a report on anomalous phenomena. It is an institutional history: The Central Intelligence Agency and Overhead Reconnaissance: The U-2 and OXCART Programs, 1954-1974, written by the agency's own historians Gregory Pedlow and Donald Welzenbach.

It was drafted in 1992 and circulated internally in classified form. In June 2013, answering a FOIA request filed by the National Security Archive at George Washington University, the CIA released a version with far fewer redactions than any before it. That version contained the site's name and a map placing it at Groom Lake, Nevada.

The global coverage that followed was, in large part, wrong. A great many headlines presented the release as a UFO-related declassification. It was nothing of the kind. It was the administrative history of two spy plane programmes.

That misreading has a long half-life. When the third PURSUE tranche landed in June 2026, much of the coverage again treated the CIA's admissions about spy planes as a fresh revelation — we covered that tranche in full, and the underlying study had by then been public for thirteen years.

Why the base is where it is

The origin is a specific Cold War problem: the United States needed photographs of the Soviet interior and had no way to take them without being shot down. The solution was the U-2, an aircraft able to fly above 70,000 feet — beyond the reach of fighters and, it was believed, of Soviet missiles.

Testing an aircraft like that required somewhere remote, with reliable weather, a natural runway on a dry lakebed, and above all no onlookers. The site chosen in 1955 beside Groom Lake met all of it, and sat inside a nuclear test range that nobody approached for obvious reasons.

The second major programme was OXCART, the development of the A-12 — forerunner of the SR-71 Blackbird — capable of more than three times the speed of sound at 80,000 feet. It was tested there from 1962.

The document also confirms a third chapter long rumoured: the evaluation of Soviet MiG fighters acquired by various means, flown and studied at the site to establish what the adversary could actually do.

The part that matters for UFO history, which almost nobody reports

The CIA's historians state explicitly that U-2 flights produced a wave of sightings in the mid-1950s. The reason is straightforward physics. Commercial aircraft of the period flew between 10,000 and 20,000 feet; military aircraft rarely exceeded 40,000. A U-2 at 70,000 feet, at dawn or dusk, was still catching sunlight when the ground below had gone dark, and its silver wings reflected that light as a bright point that appeared to hang still or move strangely.

Air traffic controllers fielded reports from commercial pilots about bright objects at impossible altitudes. Those reports reached Project Blue Book, whose analysts quietly cross-checked the sightings against the classified programme's flight logs and, when they matched, closed the cases with conventional explanations — atmospheric phenomena, ice crystals, astronomical observations — that they knew to be false, but that preserved the secret.

A word on the number, because this is where the reporting usually goes wrong. The figure most often quoted — that these flights accounted for more than half of all UFO reports in the late 1950s and 1960s — does not come from the Pedlow and Welzenbach study. It comes from a separate piece by CIA historian Gerald Haines, CIA's Role in the Study of UFOs, 1947-90, published in the agency's internal journal Studies in Intelligence in 1997. Researchers have argued the proportion is inflated. The mechanism, however, is documented by the agency itself, and the mechanism is the substantive point.

Both sides of the argument lose something here

The honest reading of this episode is uncomfortable for each of the usual positions.

For skeptics: it is documented that agencies of the U.S. government deliberately issued false explanations for sightings, for years, to protect classified programmes. The suspicion that an official account may not be sincere is not paranoia. It has a confirmed, written precedent.

For proponents of the extraterrestrial hypothesis: the same documentation shows that the thing being covered up was entirely terrestrial human technology. The secrecy was real. It protected spy planes, not craft from elsewhere.

Both conclusions hold simultaneously, and that is precisely the lesson of the case: the existence of a cover-up tells you nothing about the nature of what was covered up.

Where the alien story actually came from

The popular association between this site and non-human technology comes from no document at all. It comes from a television interview.

In May 1989, KLAS-TV in Las Vegas broadcast the account of a man initially presented under a pseudonym, interviewed by reporter George Knapp, who claimed to have worked at a nearby location called S-4 performing reverse engineering on craft of non-human origin.

That man was Robert Lazar. His account — the discs, element 115, a propulsion system based on amplified gravity — is the direct source of nearly all contemporary mythology about the base, and its cultural influence is hard to overstate.

The problems with the testimony are equally well known. Lazar said he had studied at MIT and Caltech; neither institution has any record of him, and no academic documentation supporting those credentials has surfaced. His employment history at the national laboratory he could be tied to turned out to correspond to a contractor post, not the scientific role he described. And element 115, later synthesised by nuclear physics teams, proved to be an extraordinarily unstable superheavy element with none of the properties he attributed to it.

His defenders argue the records could have been erased as part of the secrecy itself. That claim cannot be falsified: any absence of evidence is reinterpreted as evidence of a cover-up. A hypothesis armoured against all checking is the same structure that keeps the Majestic-12 documents in circulation forty years after they were shown to be fabricated — and it is what places both outside the territory of the verifiable.

What is still secret, and why that is unremarkable

The base remains active and remains classified. Its airspace is restricted, its land perimeter is posted with warnings about the use of deadly force, and employees arrive on scheduled flights from Las Vegas in unmarked aircraft.

What changed is that it can now be named. Since 2013 the government acknowledges its existence, and references have since appeared in budget and environmental documents. A 1990s lawsuit brought by former workers over exposure to toxic waste burned on the site had already forced partial admissions before the 2013 release.

That it stays secret is not surprising in the least. It is a test facility for classified aircraft, and that function has not ended. The aircraft being flown there today will probably be news in twenty years, exactly as the stealth aircraft developed in the same desert once was.

The thirteen-year gap between the 2013 release and the 2026 coverage that treated it as new is worth holding onto. It is the clearest available demonstration of a pattern this archive keeps producing: the document was never the bottleneck. Reading it was.
